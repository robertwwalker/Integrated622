Five ways data lies

Real organizational data is messy. That's not a failure of your project; it's the normal condition. These are the five ways it most often misleads, each with an example from each sector.

The wayWhat happensNonprofitPublicPrivate
MissingValues are absent, and why they're absent mattersFollow-up surveys come back only from clients who stayed in the programIncome is left blank most often by self-employed applicantsCustomer email missing for most walk-in sales
MismeasuredWhat's recorded isn't the concept"Meals served" counts meals prepared"Response time" starts at dispatch, not at the call"Satisfaction" is measured as the absence of complaints
RedefinedThe definition changed partway through"Active volunteer" changed from one shift a quarter to one a monthA new case system changed when a case counts as closedTwo divisions compute turnover differently
SelectedOnly some cases make it into the dataSuccess stories come only from graduatesOnly applicants who finished the process are surveyedOnly customers who chose to post leave reviews
MistimedDates are shifted, backfilled, or batchedGifts recorded when the check clears, not when pledgedRecords backfilled at fiscal year-endSales pulled into the quarter to meet a target

The two tracks tend to know different ones. Operations people know redefined and mistimed from process data; organizational people know selected and mismeasured from surveys. Bring your best example from work.

Simulation: if my model is right, what would the data look like?

Simulation is not fabrication. It's a rigorous tradition in supply chain, risk, and public health. You state your model, generate data that follows it, and look at what that data would look like. The intellectual work is in the model.

The teaching point: you must specify noise

A true model still produces messy data. If your simulated data falls on a perfect line, you've simulated a world that doesn't exist. Messy data is what a correct model looks like in practice.

The rules of honest simulation

  1. Declare it. Say plainly that the data is simulated.
  2. State every assumption: units, variables, relationships, and noise.
  3. Never tune it toward the answer you want. Set it from your model, then accept what comes out.

A specification has four parts

Units: each row is [one employee in one month / one site in one week / …] Variables: [each variable and its kind] Relationships: [what affects what, in which direction, and roughly how much] Noise: [how much variation the model doesn't explain; any missing values]

You'll build your own at Online 2, with a prompt that turns this specification into a dataset.

Describing a variable: the five questions

#QuestionWhat to look at
1What kind of thing is it?The six kinds. This decides everything below.
2How many?How many values, and how many are blank
3Where is the middle?Median and, when it makes sense, the mean
4How spread out?The range, and the typical distance from the middle
5What shape?A histogram: the picture of one variable

Mean or median? One big donor

Nine donors give $50 each and one gives $10,000. The mean gift is $1,045; the median gift is $50. Which one describes a typical donor? When one value is far from the rest, the median tells the truth and the mean tells you about the outlier.

Spread, in words

"Typical distance from the middle" is what statisticians call the standard deviation. You'll see the name; you never need the formula.

The kind decides the summary

KindSummarize withWatch out for
LabelsCounts and sharesThere is no mean of a label
Ordered labelsCounts by level; the medianA mean is a choice you must defend
Yes/NoThe proportion who said yesSay "proportion," not "average"

MeasurementsMedian, mean, spread, histogramOutliers pulling the mean

The AI produces every number. Your job is to read it and say whether it matches what your model predicted.
