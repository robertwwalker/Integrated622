The one idea, and where this module fits

We open with two or three of the unfamiliar references you brought from "Models in Science": what Gemini said, and whether it held up when you checked. Your AI analyst is usually right and sometimes confidently wrong; tonight is the first time we catch it.

An MBA is strategy, and strategy is built from models. Finance, accounting, operations, organizational management, marketing, HR, information systems: each is a simplified picture of one part of the organization. Strategy links them into a coherent whole, and coherent strategy is what you get when the models agree.

Data is the measurement of models. Every model makes predictions; data is where they meet reality. And a statistical model is a model too: a model of how the data came to be. Your project holds both. You bring the model of the organization; this course builds the model of the data, runs it forward (simulation) and backward (analysis). And models do two jobs: they let us test what we believe against data, and they let us forecast what will happen if we act. Managers live on the second. More: Models and the MBA.

Your Project Brief is, in effect, the theory section of a research paper. Each module writes the next section:

Project BriefTheoryHow does this part of the organization work?
Module 1Data & MethodsWhat would I measure, and where does it live?

What kind of thing is this? Six kinds of variables

Most organizational data you care about is a category or an ordered category, not a number. That's true, and it's good news: it tells you what you can honestly do with it.

KindWhat it isExamplesWhat you can do
LabelsNames or categories, no orderdepartment, sector, program type, countyCount them, take shares. You can't average them.
Ordered labelsCategories with an order; distances unknownsatisfaction 1–5, performance tier, donor level, case priorityCounts by level, the median. A mean is a choice you must defend.
Yes/NoTwo valuesleft the organization, grant awarded, case closedThe share who said yes. A label becomes a number as a proportion.
CountsHow many of something; zero means nonecases handled, volunteers, defects, visitsTotals, averages, rates per person or per week.
MeasurementsAmounts on a scalerevenue, hours, wait time, cost per caseMiddles, spreads, shapes.
Dates and timesWhen something happenedhire date, fiscal year, timestampOrder, intervals, counts per period.
The classical names, for reference

Textbooks use four levels of measurement: nominal (labels, and yes/no), ordinal (ordered labels), interval, and ratio (counts and measurements, where ratio has a true zero). We use the six practical kinds because they map directly to what you can do with each.

The arguable ones, and why they're worth arguing about:

  • 8, ZIP code: it looks like a number, but averaging ZIP codes is meaningless. It's a label.
  • 7 and 15, ratings: ordered labels that organizations average all the time. Is the gap between 1 and 2 the same as between 4 and 5? If not, the mean is a choice you must defend.
  • 13, days to decision: a count of days or a measurement of time? Either is defensible. What matters is that you can average it.
  • 14, fiscal year: a date, or an ordered label? It depends on whether you use it to put things in order or to group them.
  • 20, defects per batch: a count. Divide by batch size and it becomes a rate, which is a measurement.

The pattern to notice: organizational questions tend to live in ordered labels, and operational questions in counts and measurements. A management question usually needs both.

Where does data live? What is a row?

Wherever data lives, it ends up as a table: one row per unit, one column per variable. "What is a row?" is the most important question in this module.

Where organizational data lives

  • HR and payroll systems; CRM and donor databases; case-management systems; finance and ERP systems; electronic records
  • Surveys, documents, and email, which are unstructured until someone codes them
  • And spreadsheets: the real system of record in most small organizations

Three sectors, three data landscapes

Public

Mandated reporting, open-data portals, administrative records. Rich, but bound by privacy rules.

Nonprofit

Donor and grant data are rich; outcome data are thin. Funders often define the variables.

Private

Transaction data are abundant. Confidentiality and departmental silos limit access.

Unstructured becomes structured by coding

Twenty open-ended survey comments become five themes, and the theme becomes a Labels column. AI can do the first pass; you decide whether the themes are right.

Operationalization: from concept to variable

Walk this chain for every concept in your model:

concept → what would you see if it were high? if it were low? → what gets recorded? → what kind of thing is it? → where does it live?

Pod exercise: the Skeptic's Chair (40 minutes)

Each member takes a turn as Author. Everyone else takes a role, and the roles rotate, so you sit in every chair once.

RoleJobTime
AuthorStates one concept from their Brief and their prediction about it2 min
TranslatorProposes a variable for that concept and names its kind3 min
SkepticNames what that variable misses and what could go wrong. Numbers don't capture everything is exactly the Skeptic's job.3 min
ScribeRecords the variable and the objections; the Author gets the last word2 min

Afterward, the Author writes their variable table, taking the Skeptic's objections into account: concept → variable → kind → source → what it misses.
