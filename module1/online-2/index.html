The Data & Methods draft

One to two pages. It becomes the Data & Methods section of your white paper, so it is not extra work. Copy the outline into your own document and fill it in as you work through the steps below.

DATA & METHODS (draft) 1. Unit of observation One row of my data is ______, because ______. 2. Variable table (3 to 6 rows) Concept | Variable | Kind | Source | What it misses 3. Data source Real: the system, my access, what it covers, the time period. Simulated: units, variables, relationships, noise, and this sentence: "These data are simulated from the model described above." 4. Quality concerns The way my data is most likely to mislead (missing, mismeasured, redefined, selected, or mistimed), and what I will do about it. 5. First description Two or three key variables, each described with the five questions, and one sentence each: does it look like my model predicted?

Due on Canvas before your Module 2 kick-off: Tue Nov 3, 6:00 pm (Salem) or Wed Nov 4, 6:00 pm (Portland). Your pod reads it first.

Work session: five steps

Replace anything in [brackets] with your own project's details. The AI does the arithmetic; every sentence in your draft about what it means is yours.

1

No data yet? Build an honest simulation

Skip this step if you have a real sample.

I'm simulating data for my project. My model, in plain words: [your model]. Each row is [your unit, e.g., one employee in one month]. The variables are [each variable and its kind]. The relationships I expect are [direction and rough size of each]. The data should be realistically messy: [how noisy, any missing values]. Build a dataset of about [number] rows that follows this model, give it to me as a CSV, and then list every assumption you made.

Does your simulation contain noise? A true model still produces messy data. If every point falls on a perfect line, ask for more realistic variation.

The rules of honest simulation
  • Declare it. Say plainly in your draft that the data is simulated.
  • State every assumption. Ask the AI to list them, and keep the list in your draft.
  • Never tune it toward the answer you want. Set the relationships from your model and your prior course, then accept what comes out.
2

What is a row?

What does one row of my data represent? How many rows are there, and how many different [people / sites / cases] do they cover?

Is one row the unit your model is about? If your model is about people but a row is a person-month, say how you'll handle that.

3

Describe two or three key variables

Describe [variable]: what kind of variable it is, how many values there are including blanks, the middle (the median, and the mean only if a mean makes sense for this kind of variable), how spread out it is, and its shape, with a chart. Tell me any choices you made, such as dropping blanks or zeros.

Did the AI make a choice for you, like dropping blanks, averaging a rating, or treating zeros as "none"? If so, would you make the same choice?

4

Where could your data mislead you?

My data comes from [the system or survey, or "a simulation"]. For each of these, say whether it could affect my data and what I would see in the data if it were happening: missing values, mismeasurement, a definition that changed, only some cases recorded, dates or entries shifted.

Which one is most likely for your data? That one goes in section 4 of your draft, with what you'll do about it.

5

Hold it up against your prediction

My model predicts [your prediction from the Project Brief]. Based only on the descriptions above, does the data look the way the model says it should? What can a description of one variable at a time not tell me?

Describing one variable at a time can't show you a relationship. That's Module 2. Write one honest sentence for each variable: is it consistent with your model, inconsistent, or not yet able to say?

Roundtable reflection: AI and the State of Oregon

Submit on Canvas, separately from your pod postDue Sun Nov 1, 11:59 pm250–400 wordsCounts toward participation

Nik Blosser became Oregon's first Chief Privacy Officer and AI Strategist in 2025, and now leads the state's AI strategy, governance, and adoption as its Chief AI Officer. A state government adopting AI is an organization making a change, which is exactly what your Project Brief evaluates. Listen to him the way you'd read a Brief.

While you listen

Jot down three things: one goal he names for AI in state government, one worry or risk he raises, and one claim you'd want to see evidence for.

Then write

Answer two of these five questions, then write one question you would have asked him and name the course idea it comes from.

  1. A model of the state. Every model has concepts, relationships, assumptions, and predictions (Models and the MBA). What model of state government, and of AI's place in it, did he describe? Which of the four parts was thinnest?
  2. How would Oregon know it worked? Take one goal he named through the operationalization chain: what you'd see if it were high or low, what gets recorded, what kind of thing it is, and where it lives. Once that number becomes someone's target, how could it drift away from the goal? (Campbell and Goodhart, in the kick-off's optional reading.)
  3. Capacity that isn't people. Projects at the boundary asks what changes when some of the work is done by AI. When an agent does part of a state agency's work, who is accountable for it, what records does it write, and what would redefined data look like?
  4. What gets counted counts. Whose data does state AI run on? With D'Ignazio and Klein in mind, when would an Oregonian want to be in that data, and when would they rather not be? Compare the rule we follow for workplace data in Gemini.
  5. The jagged frontier. Which state tasks did he treat as inside AI's frontier, and which as outside? Would Mollick draw the line in the same place?
Can't attend? Watch the Roundtable's own recording if one is posted; Canvas will say. If not, read the summary, vision, and principles of the State Government Artificial Intelligence Advisory Council's Final Recommended Action Plan (February 2025), delivered to Governor Kotek, and KATU's interview with Blosser. Answer the same questions about the plan and the interview.

We open the Module 2 kick-off with a few of your reflections. Everything he described is a decision made with data; Module 2 is about what data can show.

Before you submit: the common failures

These are the problems that show up most often in Data & Methods drafts. Check yours against each one.

FailureWhat it looks likeThe fix
Wrong rowThe draft talks about people, but a row is a person-quarter; or two tables were combined and the row count jumped.State the unit in section 1, and check row counts before and after any join.
Wrong kindA 1–5 rating reported as "3.47"; an ID number averaged; a yes/no column described as a "mean" without calling it a proportion.Name each variable's kind in your table, and summarize it the way that kind allows.
Silent choicesBlanks or zeros dropped without saying so.Report how many were blank or zero, and say what you did with them.
Simulation with no noisePerfectly straight relationships, round numbers, no outliers.Ask for realistic variation, and say how noisy you assumed reality is.
Simulation tuned to the answerSettings changed until the data "showed" the prediction.Fix the settings from your model first, and report what came out.
Numbers without meaningA pasted AI summary with no sentence about the prediction.Every description ends with your sentence: consistent, inconsistent, or can't tell yet.
