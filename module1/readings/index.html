Module 1 · Readings

Module 1 readings

About two hours of reading before each session, with an active reading guide for every reading.

How to use these guides

Each reading comes with a guide in three parts. The guide is the point: read with a question in hand, and finish with something you can use in class or in your project.

Before you read

A few minutes. Write down a prediction or a question from your own project, so you read with a purpose.

While you read

Four or five things to find. Answer each in a line or two of your own words, not by copying.

After you read

A short piece of writing for your own project. Bring it to the session; it feeds your pod work and your drafts.

Each session page also offers a few optional videos or podcasts from business, government, or nonprofit settings, under "Optional: watch or listen." Keep your answers in one running document, your reading notebook, so you can find them when you write the Data & Methods draft. Each session's set takes about two hours, guides included. The times below are estimates; some of these readings are dense, and slow is fine.

Read ahead. Everything is posted now. Each in-person night is followed closely by a Thursday session: two days for Salem, one for Portland. Portland especially: do the Online 1 and Online 2 readings before your Wednesday nights, not after.

Unfamiliar references: read with your AI analyst

Some of these readings assume things you haven't met: a name, a model, a term from another field. Don't skip them. Mark every one, then ask Gemini about the ones that matter. Then check one of its answers against a second source. Your AI analyst is often right and sometimes confidently wrong, and these readings are a low-stakes place to practice catching it.

I'm reading [title and author] for an MBA course on data and decision science. I ran into this reference and don't know it: [the name, model, or term, with the sentence it appears in]. Explain what it is in plain language, why the author might mention it here, and give me one source I could check your answer against.

Open readings are free online. Course site readings are on this site. Canvas reserve readings are on course reserve; Canvas shows where. Hubbard's How to Measure Anything is also available in full as a Willamette University Library ebook, on a 365-day loan; check it out once and it covers every Hubbard chapter here. The guides follow the third edition (2014); if you have the second, the chapter numbers are the same and the titles differ slightly.

Read before the Module 1 kick-off · Tue Oct 20 (Salem) · Wed Oct 21 (Portland)

What is a model, and how do you measure what's in one?

The course rests on one idea: data is the measurement of models. Tonight's readings take each half of that in turn. First, what a model is and how anyone learns from one. Then, how a concept inside a model becomes something you can observe. Read them in this order. Your Project Brief is due the same night, so start early; the Brief and these readings help each other.

1. Models and the MBA

This site's Models and the MBA page.

CoreCourse siteAbout 15 minutes
While

Find these

  1. The four parts every model has. Check that your Brief has all four. Which is thinnest?
  2. The two jobs a model does. Which one does your project's question ask of your model?
  3. The two models every project holds. Write one sentence naming each for your project.

2. Frigg and Hartmann: models in science

Roman Frigg and Stephan Hartmann, "Models in Science," Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Fall 2013 edition). Read sections 1, 3, and 5.2 only.

CoreOpenAbout 60 minutes, including Gemini

Why this reading. It is a hard read, written for philosophers, and that is part of the point. The Models page tells you what a model is in our terms; this entry shows how people who think about models for a living sort them out. Two of its ideas carry the whole course: there are models of phenomena and models of data, and you learn about the world from a model in two steps, not one.

Before

Your own definition (3 minutes)

Write one sentence: "A model is…" Don't look anything up. You'll revise it at the end.

While

Find these

  1. Section 1: two kinds of representation. The entry distinguishes models that represent some part of the world from models of data. Find both. Which one is your Project Brief? Which one does Module 1 ask you to build?
  2. Section 3: learning in two steps. The entry says we first learn about the model itself, and then have to carry what we learned over to the thing it represents. Find where simulation comes in. What has to be true for the carrying-over to work? (Compare element 2 of your Brief: "what would have to be true for the model to be right?")
  3. Section 5.2: a debate. Name the question being argued about, in one sentence, and the main positions. Now picture an organization that holds several models of itself (finance, operations, organizational) that don't agree. Does the debate help you think about that?
  4. Unfamiliar references. As you read, mark every name, model, or term you don't recognize. You'll find plenty. Pick three, ask Gemini about each with the prompt at the top of this page, and check one answer against a second source.
After

Revise, and bring three references (10 minutes)

Rewrite your "A model is…" sentence. What changed? Then write down your three references, Gemini's one-line answers, and what your check turned up: confirmed, corrected, or couldn't verify. Bring them to the kick-off; we'll open with a few.

3. Hubbard: why immeasurables aren't

Douglas W. Hubbard, How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of "Intangibles" in Business, 3rd ed. (Wiley, 2014), chapter 3, "The Illusion of Intangibles: Why Immeasurables Aren't."

CoreCanvas reserve · library ebookAbout 50 minutes

Why this reading. Your Brief is full of concepts that feel unmeasurable: engagement, readiness, quality, fairness. Hubbard argues that if something matters, it shows up in something you can observe. His "clarification chain" is the first half of the operationalization chain we walk on the first night.

Before

Pick your hardest concept (5 minutes)

Choose the one concept in your Brief you think will be hardest to measure. Write one sentence: why can't it be measured?

While

Find these

  1. His definition of measurement. It is not "an exact number." Put it in your own words. What does it ask of you that you could do right now?
  2. Three misunderstandings. Hubbard says things seem immeasurable because we misunderstand the concept of measurement, the object being measured, or the methods. Which one is your sentence from "Before" an example of?
  3. The clarification chain. Line it up against ours: concept → what you'd see if it were high or low → what gets recorded → what kind of thing it is → where it lives. Which steps do the two chains share? Which of ours does he leave out?
  4. Where the Skeptic would object. Hubbard is an optimist. Mark one claim that someone who says "numbers don't capture everything" would push back on. Is the objection fair?
After

Walk your concept through the chain (15 minutes)

In three or four lines, take your hardest concept through the clarification chain. End with one observable thing that would be more common if your concept were high. Bring it to the kick-off: it is your first turn as Author in the Skeptic's Chair.

A connection to look for: Hubbard treats measurement as reducing uncertainty for a decision. That is the forecasting job of a model on the Models page.

Optional: an AI tour of the models in your discipline (about 20 minutes)

Last spring I asked Gemini to survey the models used across the business disciplines, and to sort models in twenty academic fields into explanatory and predictive. Do the same for the discipline you're arriving from:

Give a detailed discussion of how models are used in [organizational management / operations management]. Give at least four common models, and for each: its key variables, the relationships it claims between them, whether it is mainly explanatory or predictive, and a reference I could follow. Double-check your references.
  1. Is the model your Brief uses on the list? If not, why not?
  2. Check two of its references. Do they exist, and do they say what Gemini says they say?
  3. Pick one model on the list. What would you have to measure to test it?
Optional: Campbell and Goodhart on what happens to a measure once it matters (about 45 minutes)

Two famous statements of one problem, arrived at separately: the social psychologist's and the economist's. Read them together. They are the Skeptic's best weapons, and they come back at In-person 2 (mismeasured), in the Projects reading at Online 2, and in Module 3.

Campbell's Law

Donald T. Campbell, "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change," Occasional Paper No. 8, Public Affairs Center, Dartmouth College (1976). Reprinted, open access, in the Journal of MultiDisciplinary Evaluation 7, no. 15 (2011): article page and PDF · DOI. The paper is long; read only the passage on the corruption of quantitative indicators (search the PDF for "quantitative social indicator") and the examples around it. About 20 minutes.

  1. Restate Campbell's Law in one sentence of your own.
  2. Find his example of crime statistics under political pressure. What did the measure reward, and what did people start doing?
  3. Riverbend adopted SmartShift to reduce overtime and turnover, and leadership wants to know whether it worked. If site managers knew they would be judged on those two numbers, how could the numbers drift away from what they are supposed to measure?

Goodhart's Law, and its four kinds

David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant, "Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law," arXiv:1803.04585 (2018; revised 2019). Open access. Read the introduction and the plain-language description of each of the four variants; skip the formal notation and the material on AI alignment. About 25 minutes.

Background: the economist Charles Goodhart made the original observation in 1975, about British monetary policy. The version everyone quotes, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure," is the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's 1997 restatement.

  1. Four ways a measure fails as a target. The authors name four: regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial. Put each in one plain sentence.
  2. The causal variant. A measure can move with the goal without causing it; push on the measure and the goal doesn't follow. That gap between moving together and causing is what Module 3 is about. Write down one example from your own organization now; you'll need it then.
  3. The adversarial variant. People, or AI agents, who know the target will work on the target. Compare with Campbell. Is it the same law?
  4. Your own project. Pick the variable in your Brief most likely to become someone's target. Which of the four variants threatens it most?

For a managerial case, Michael Harris and Bill Tayler, "Don't Let Metrics Undermine Your Business," Harvard Business Review (September–October 2019), through the library, tells the Wells Fargo cross-selling story and names the habit of mistaking the metric for the strategy: surrogation. To hear the case instead, the kick-off page links HBR's Cold Call podcast episode on it.

Read before Online 1 · Thu Oct 22

What a good table looks like, and what an AI analyst does with it

Thursday's question is "what is a row?", asked of real files with an AI analyst. The first two readings answer it from the side of the table. The last two are about the analyst: where AI helps and where it quietly fails, and why.

1. Broman and Woo: data organization in spreadsheets

Karl W. Broman and Kara H. Woo, "Data Organization in Spreadsheets," The American Statistician 72, no. 1 (2018): 2–10. Journal version · Free preprint.

CoreOpenAbout 40 minutes

Why this reading. In most small organizations the spreadsheet is the system of record, and your AI analyst reads a table exactly as it is laid out. Skip anything about specific software; the principles are what matter.

Before

Look at a real table (5 minutes)

Open staff_quarters.csv in Google Sheets and scroll for two minutes. Write one sentence: what is one row?

While

Find these

  1. "Make it a rectangle." What do they mean? In their examples, what is one row, and what is one column?
  2. Empty cells. What do they recommend instead of leaving a cell blank? Now look at Riverbend's engagement and exit_reason columns. Does a blank there mean "no answer," "doesn't apply," or something else? How would you know?
  3. "Create a data dictionary." Compare their advice with the Riverbend data dictionary. Name one thing they would want that Riverbend's dictionary doesn't tell you.
  4. Color and highlighting as data. Think of a spreadsheet at work where a color, a merged cell, or a note carries meaning. What would an AI analyst lose when it reads that file?
  5. Dates. Find their rule for writing dates. Check Riverbend's hire_date: does it follow it?
After

One rule your data breaks (10 minutes)

For your project's organization, real or a useful fiction: which of their rules is its data most likely to break, and what would an AI analyst get wrong because of it? Two or three sentences. You'll use this in the data audit and in Part 1 of the Module 1 exercise.

2. Wickham: tidy data

Hadley Wickham, "Tidy Data," Journal of Statistical Software 59, no. 10 (2014): 1–23. Read sections 1 through 3; skip the later sections on software and the code.

CoreOpenAbout 40 minutes

Why this reading. Broman and Woo say how to keep a table clean. Wickham says what a table is: every variable a column, every observation a row, and every kind of observational unit its own table. That last rule is why Riverbend comes as two files, and why joining them carelessly goes so wrong.

Before

Why two files? (3 minutes)

Riverbend has one file of staff and one of sites. Write one sentence: why not put everything in one file?

While

Find these

  1. The three rules of tidy data. Copy them in your own words. Does each Riverbend file follow all three?
  2. "Observational unit." This is Wickham's term for what we mean by "what is a row?" What are the observational units in Riverbend? Is "one employee" one of them?
  3. Column headers that are values. Find his example where the column names are themselves data. Think of a report at work laid out that way, such as months across the top. What question becomes hard to ask of it?
  4. Several kinds of unit in one table. Find what goes wrong when one table mixes kinds of units. Then predict what happens if you join staff_quarters to site_weeks on site_id without first reducing site_weeks to one row per site. We'll check your prediction on Thursday.
After

Your project's units (10 minutes)

Name the observational unit or units in your project's data. If there is more than one, how many tables would you need, and what column would link them? This is section 1 of your Data & Methods draft, in rough form.

3. Mollick: the jagged frontier

Ethan Mollick, "Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier," One Useful Thing, September 16, 2023. A plain-language summary of a field experiment with management consultants using AI.

CoreOpenAbout 15 minutes

Why this reading. On Thursday the AI does the arithmetic and you make the choices. This is the best short evidence on why that division of labor matters: AI helps a great deal on some tasks and quietly hurts on others that look just like them.

While

Find these

  1. What is the "jagged frontier"? Why is it hard to see from inside a task?
  2. What happened to the consultants who used AI on the task that lay outside the frontier?
  3. What are centaurs and cyborgs? Which do you expect to be on Thursday?
After

Your tripwire (5 minutes)

Write one sentence: "I'll know I've crossed the frontier with my AI analyst when…" Watch for it on Thursday: in the engagement and caseload demos, the AI's numbers are right, and it still makes choices only you can judge.

4. Mollick: thinking like an AI

Ethan Mollick, "Thinking Like an AI," One Useful Thing, October 20, 2024. A short, plain-language account of how a large language model produces an answer.

CoreOpenAbout 20 minutes

Why this reading. The jagged frontier shows where AI fails; this piece helps explain why. An AI language model is itself a model, a model of text, and knowing a little about how it works tells you when to trust your AI analyst and when to check it. On Thursday you will ask Gemini about two files; this is what is happening on the other side.

Before

Your working theory (3 minutes)

Write one sentence: when you ask Gemini a question about a spreadsheet, what do you think it is actually doing?

While

Find these

  1. Next-token prediction. In your own words, what is the model doing when it answers you? Why does that make fluent, confident answers cheap, and checked answers not?
  2. Memory. What does he say about what the model can hold in mind at once? What does that mean for a long Gemini chat where you uploaded two files an hour ago?
  3. An AI is a model too. Using the four parts of a model from the Models page, what are its concepts, its assumptions, its predictions? Where would you expect it to be confidently wrong about a table of data?
After

Two checks you'll always run (5 minutes)

Revise your "Before" sentence. Then write the two checks you will run on every answer your AI analyst gives you this term, such as counting rows yourself or asking it to show its steps. Bring them Thursday; the demo will test them.

Read before In-person 2 · Tue Oct 27 (Salem) · Wed Oct 28 (Portland)

How data misleads, and what a single number hides

In-person 2 covers the five ways data lies, honest simulation, and describing a variable. The first reading is about the categories data is built from. The other two are about why one number, usually an average, is not enough, and what a simulation gives you instead.

1. D'Ignazio and Klein: what gets counted counts

Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein, Data Feminism (MIT Press, 2020), chapter 4, "What Gets Counted Counts." Free online under a Creative Commons license.

CoreOpenAbout 60 minutes

Why this reading. Every Labels column starts with someone deciding the categories. Who decides shapes who shows up in the data and how. This is selected and mismeasured, two of the five ways data lies, at their root. The examples come mostly from gender and public data; read them as cases of a general problem every organization has.

Before

Who chose the boxes? (5 minutes)

Look at Riverbend's role, age_band, and exit_reason columns. Who decided those categories? Who might not fit them well?

While

Find these

  1. The opening claim. The chapter opens with a claim about what happens to things that are counted and things that are not. Rewrite it as a sentence about your own organization.
  2. A form with too few boxes. Find an example where a form or database had fewer categories than the people it described. What happened to the people who didn't fit? Which of our six kinds of variable was involved?
  3. Counting can also protect. The authors don't argue against classifying. Find where they show that being counted can matter, and where it can expose people. When would someone want to be in your data, and when would they rather not be?
  4. A definition that changed. Note one place where a category or definition changed over time. That is redefined, another of the five ways data lies.
After

Audit one category (10 minutes)

Pick one Labels or Ordered labels variable from your variable table. In three or four sentences: who set its categories, who might not fit, and what you would lose by collapsing or splitting them. This goes straight into the "what it misses" column.

Operations track: product categories, defect codes, and downtime reason codes are classifications too. Someone chose them, and the choice decides which problems are visible.

2. Savage: the flaw of averages

Sam L. Savage, "The Flaw of Averages," Harvard Business Review 80, no. 11 (November 2002): 20–21. Two pages: free PDF.

CoreOpenAbout 10 minutes

Why this reading. In class, the mean-versus-median donor example is a problem of describing data. Savage shows the same trap as a problem of planning, and why a simulation that includes noise is the honest answer to it.

While

Find these

  1. Retell the opening joke as a sentence about your own organization.
  2. Find the example where a plan built on average demand goes wrong. Why does the plan do worse than the average suggests it will?
  3. What does he recommend instead of a single number?

3. Hubbard: quantifying risk through modeling

Hubbard, How to Measure Anything, 3rd ed., chapter 6, "Quantifying Risk through Modeling."

CoreLibrary ebookAbout 45 minutes

Why this reading. Savage names the problem; Hubbard shows the method. A Monte Carlo simulation states a model, puts a range on each uncertain input, and runs the model forward many times to see the range of outcomes. That is exactly the honest simulation you'll specify at In-person 2 and build at Online 2, done by hand so you can see its parts. Skim the section on software tools; your AI analyst replaces them.

Before

Your single number (5 minutes)

Name the one number in your project someone would be tempted to plan on: an average wait, an average caseload, an average score. Write it down with your best guess of its value.

While

Find these

  1. How not to quantify risk. What does Hubbard say is wrong with the way organizations usually express risk? Which of our six kinds of variable is a "high / medium / low" rating?
  2. The worked example. Identify its inputs, the range given to each, the relationship that turns inputs into the outcome, and what comes out. Match each to our four-part simulation specification: units, variables, relationships, noise.
  3. Ranges, not points. How does he express what we don't know about each input? Where would those ranges come from in your project?
  4. The risk paradox. State it in one sentence. Does it describe any decision in your organization?
After

From one number to a range (10 minutes)

Go back to your single number. Replace it with a range you are 90 percent sure contains the true value, and name two inputs that drive it, each with its own range. That is the "noise" line of your simulation specification. Bring it to the simulation segment at 7:15.

Optional: a Monte Carlo simulation with Gemini (about 15 minutes)

Last spring's Monte Carlo writeup asks Gemini, in one plain-language paragraph, to simulate customers arriving, deciding whether to buy, and spending. Read the prompt, then write your own version of it for the two inputs you named above. Don't run it yet; we'll do that at Online 2.

Read before Online 2 · Thu Oct 29

Documenting your data, and holding it up against your model

At Online 2 you draft the Data & Methods section. The first reading is a checklist for documenting a dataset. The second asks where the change you are studying came from, and what it did to the data. The third is a classic on how a model and data talk to each other, and on which of a model's flaws are worth worrying about.

1. Gebru et al.: datasheets for datasets

Timnit Gebru, Jamie Morgenstern, Briana Vecchione, Jennifer Wortman Vaughan, Hanna Wallach, Hal Daumé III, and Kate Crawford, "Datasheets for Datasets," Communications of the ACM 64, no. 12 (2021): 86–92. Journal version · Free version.

CoreOpenAbout 40 minutes

Why this reading. The authors propose that every dataset come with a datasheet: a standard set of questions about why it was made, what is in it, how it was collected, and what it should and shouldn't be used for. Your Data & Methods draft is a short datasheet for your own data. The paper comes from machine learning; ignore the technical asides and read it as a documentation checklist.

Before

Reread your variable table (5 minutes)

Have your variable table and data-source decision from In-person 2 open. Keep the Data & Methods outline next to it.

While

Find these

  1. The analogy. Why do the authors borrow the idea from electronic components? What goes wrong when a dataset has no datasheet?
  2. Map the sections. The questions are grouped into sections: motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing and cleaning, uses, distribution, and maintenance. For each of the five sections of your Data & Methods draft, find the datasheet section that matches it best.
  3. "What is a row?" in their words. Find the composition questions about what each instance represents and whether anything is missing. Which of our ideas are they?
  4. The five ways data lies. Which collection-process questions would catch data that is selected or mistimed?
  5. What our outline leaves out. Find one datasheet question with no counterpart in our outline that matters for your project.
After

Answer five questions about your own data (15 minutes)

Choose five datasheet questions that our outline doesn't already ask, and answer them for your data. If your data is simulated, answer them for the simulation: who made it, from what model, under what assumptions, and what it should not be used to claim. Your answers go into sections 3 and 4 of your draft.

For the Module 1 exercise reflection: what would a datasheet for Riverbend say that its data dictionary doesn't?

2. Projects at the boundary

This site's Projects at the boundary page, drawing on Larson, Gray, and Sirisomboonsuk, Project Management: A Socio-Technical Approach (McGraw Hill, 2024).

CoreCourse siteAbout 30 minutes, with the guide

Why this reading. You have a project, and the change your Brief evaluates almost certainly arrived as one. A project decides where a change happens, whose time it uses, and who keeps measuring once it ends, and each of those leaves a mark on your data. It is also where your two prior courses meet: organizational management on one side of the boundary, operations management on the other.

Before

Is your change a project? (3 minutes)

Write down the change your Brief evaluates. Who authorized it? Whose people carried it out? If you don't know, write that down too.

While

Find these

  1. The two sides. For each data source in your variable table, which side of the boundary owns it: the organization or operations?
  2. Three interfaces. Answer the three data questions (authority, resources, integration) for your own change, as the page does for Riverbend.
  3. Setups. Is your organization functional, matrix, or projectized for the change you're studying? What does that predict about where its records live and how they're defined?
  4. Little's Law. Find something in your project that arrives, waits, and leaves: cases, orders, tickets, applications. What is its arrival rate, and what is its time in the system?
  5. Acceptance criteria. Which part of your Brief plays the role of acceptance criteria agreed at the start?
  6. Where this is going. On Pearl's ladder, which rung can your project's evidence reach right now? What would you have to add from outside the data to climb one rung higher?
  7. Optional: the new question. If you open the panel on agentic AI: If part of the work in your change were done by an AI agent, which of the three interfaces would change most, and what would your data stop seeing?
After

Before, during, after (10 minutes)

Write two sentences for your Data & Methods draft. First: who owns your data before, during, and after the change. Second: what the change itself may have done to how things are recorded. They belong in section 3 or 4. Keep them at hand for Box, who comes next: they are where your loop is most likely to close incompletely.

3. Box: science and statistics

George E. P. Box, "Science and Statistics," Journal of the American Statistical Association 71, no. 356 (1976): 791–799. Free PDF · Journal version.

CoreOpenAbout 45 minutes

Why this reading. This is the paper where Box first wrote that all models are wrong. (His more famous version, "all models are wrong, but some are useful," came a few years later.) Here, the sentence that follows is the more useful one. Box describes learning as a back-and-forth between a model and data: the model says what to look for, the data pushes back, the model improves. That is the loop between the two models of your project. Skip any passage that turns mathematical; the argument is in the prose.

Before

What's wrong with your model? (3 minutes)

Your model is wrong somewhere; every model is. Write down the one way you most suspect it's wrong.

While

Find these

  1. The loop. Find Box's description of how theory and data drive each other. Draw it as a loop and compare it with the two-models diagram on the Models page. Where does Module 1 sit on that loop? Then reread the end of Projects at the boundary: where will the loop close incompletely for the change you are evaluating?
  2. "All models are wrong." Find the sentence, and the sentence about tigers and mice. What is the tiger in your Data & Methods draft, and what are the mice?
  3. Parsimony. Why does Box prefer a simple model to an elaborate one? Look at your variable table: which variable could you drop, and what would you lose?
  4. Fisher. Box holds up R. A. Fisher as a model of how a statistician should work. What does Fisher do that Box admires? Use the unfamiliar-references prompt if you don't know who Fisher was.
After

Name your tiger (10 minutes)

Write the closing sentences for section 5 of your draft: for each key variable, one sentence saying whether it looks the way your model predicted (consistent, inconsistent, or can't tell yet). Then one more sentence naming the way your model is most importantly wrong, and what data would show it. Compare it with what you wrote under "Before."

Optional: Hubbard on clarifying the decision first (about 40 minutes)

Hubbard, How to Measure Anything, chapter 4, "Clarifying the Measurement Problem." Canvas reserve or library ebook.

  1. Hubbard says to define the decision before the measurement. What decision would your project's measurements inform, and who makes it?
  2. How does he distinguish uncertainty from risk? Which one is your white paper's forecast about?
  3. Rewrite your Brief's question (element 3) as a decision with at least two options someone could choose.
