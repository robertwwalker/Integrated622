Joining tables: a SQL FAQ

How separate tables become one, the full range of joins, and the one number that tells you whether a join went right.

In short

You will never write SQL in this course. Your AI analyst writes it. What you do is read it, and decide whether it did what you meant. The idea to carry is small: a join matches rows of two tables on a key. The kind of join decides what happens to rows with no match, and the number of rows before and after tells you whether it worked.

Almost all organizational data lives in separate tables that share an identifier: HR in one system, payroll in another, a survey in a third. Combining them is the first move in most analyses, and it is where the costliest mistakes hide, because a wrong join still returns an answer. Module 1 asks you to join Riverbend's two files; this page gives you the vocabulary for the whole family of joins.

Everything below uses two tiny tables, so you can check every row by eye.

Three more you will hear about

Semi join

A filter, not a combination: keep the rows of A that have a match in B, and bring no columns from B. "Staff whose site is in the sites table."

Cross join

Every row of A paired with every row of B. No key at all. Useful for building a grid (every site in every quarter); dangerous if it happens by accident. Here: 5 × 3 = 15 rows.

Self join

A table joined to itself, for rows that point at other rows in the same table: each employee and their manager, each case and its predecessor.

None of these three has a standard Venn picture, which is a hint about where Venn pictures stop helping (see below).

Questions and answers

Which join should I ask for?

Ask first: which table's rows am I counting? Make that table the left one and ask for a left join. You keep every row you were counting, and the blanks show you exactly which ones failed to match. Use an inner join only when unmatched rows should truly vanish, and say so out loud, because they disappear silently. Use a full join or an anti join when the question is "what doesn't line up?"

What is a "row," and why does it matter so much?

Each table has a grain: what one row stands for. In the toy tables, a row of A is one employee, and a row of B is one site. A join is only safe when you know the grain of each table and of the result you want. The rule of this course: before you join, decide what one row of the result should be. The next question shows what happens when you don't.

Why did my row count jump?

When the key is not unique in both tables, a join pairs every matching row on one side with every matching row on the other. That is a "fan-out." Suppose we join the staff to a table with one row per site per week (North has 3 weeks, South has 2, West has 1):

Three matched staff become 8 rows: North's two staff are each copied three times (2 × 3 = 6), and South's one staff member twice. Count or average anything in this table and North counts triple. The fix is to reduce the table to the grain you need before joining (here, one row per site), or to join on every column that makes a row unique. The Venn picture cannot show this: it has no way to draw a row being copied.

Rows after a join: a rule of thumb

For each key value, an inner join gives (rows with that key in A) × (rows with that key in B). Add those up. If either table has only one row per key, an inner or left join is never bigger than the other table. If both have repeats, expect it to grow. Whenever the key is more than one column (a site and a quarter, say), joining on only one of them is the same mistake as a missing key: it multiplies rows.

What happens when the key is blank?

A blank never matches anything, not even another blank. Dee has no site, so Dee drops out of an inner join, appears with a blank type in a left join, and shows up in the left anti join. Ask for the anti join to see who is falling through.

Is a bigger table a better table?

No. A join that makes the table bigger has usually done something you did not intend. The size of the result is information. A result with more rows than either input, or fewer than you expected, is the first sign to stop and look.

How to read the SQL your AI writes

You do not need to write it. You need to find three things in it. Here is a left join of the toy tables, with its three parts marked:

SELECT  s.employee, k.type
FROM    staff  AS s              -- 1. the grain: one row per employee
LEFT JOIN sites AS k             -- 2. the kind of join
        ON s.site = k.site;      -- 3. the key
  1. FROM names the table whose rows you are keeping (the grain of the result).
  2. The join word (INNER, LEFT, RIGHT, FULL) says which unmatched rows survive.
  3. ON is the key. Check that it has every column it needs, and that it is the column you meant.

Two more words you will see: GROUP BY collapses rows to a smaller grain (it is how an AI reduces weekly rows to one per site before joining), and WHERE filters rows. If you cannot tell what a step did, ask: "Explain in plain words what that step did."

Checks to run every time

  1. Count rows before and after. Each input, and the result. Can you explain the number?
  2. Check the key. How many distinct values does it have in each table? Does any value repeat in both?
  3. Look for blanks in the columns that came from the other table. Each blank is a row that failed to match.
  4. Spot-check one key by hand. Pick one site, one person, one case, and follow it through.

Prompts for your AI analyst (Gemini, signed in with your Willamette account). Replace what is in brackets:

Before joining [table A] and [table B] on [key], tell me what one row of each table represents, how many rows each has, how many distinct values of [key] each has, and whether any value of [key] appears more than once in both tables. Then say whether the join will keep, drop, or multiply rows.
Join them with a left join from [table A]. Explain in plain words what you did, show me the SQL, and tell me the number of rows in [table A], in [table B], and in the result.
Reduce [table B] to one row per [key] first, then join it to [table A]. Tell me exactly how you reduced it, and why the result has the number of rows it does.
Show me the rows of [table A] with no match in [table B], and the rows of [table B] with no match in [table A]. How many of each?

Where the Venn picture misleads

Venn diagrams show which keys survive. A join, though, produces combined rows, and when keys repeat, one row on one side is paired with several on the other. That is why the pictures say nothing about row counts, and why they cannot draw a cross join or a self join. Use the circles to remember what each join keeps, and use the row-count checks above to know what it did. One well-known critique makes the same point: Say NO to Venn Diagrams When Explaining JOINs.
