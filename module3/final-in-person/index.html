Honest accounting

"The strongest projects are not the ones that prove their model right. They are the ones that say clearly what the data establishes, what it cannot, and what it would take to know more." That's from the project page, and tonight is about saying it so a decision-maker can't miss it.

Where the limit went: the Columbia slide

In 2003, engineers told NASA managers what they knew about a foam strike on the shuttle Columbia, in a slide. The fact that mattered most, that the foam was far larger than anything that had been tested, was buried in its last bullet. Tufte's point isn't that slides are bad. It's that where you put a limitation decides whether anyone sees it.

Five rules for evidence

RuleIn your white paper
Inform, don't persuadeYour job is to help the decision-maker choose well, not to win.
Balance, not false balanceGive both sides their real weight: not equal time to a weak objection, and not silence on a strong one.
Disclose uncertaintyA range, not a point. Say what drives its width.
State the quality of the evidenceThat's the rung, and the design that got you there.
Pre-empt misunderstandingName the conclusion a reader is most likely to jump to, and say whether it's supported.

Paraphrased from Blastland, Freeman, van der Linden, Marteau, and Spiegelhalter (2020).

Two minutes, alone: open your slides. On which slide does your biggest limitation appear, and where on it? If it's the last bullet of a late slide, move it.

Premortem (15 minutes: 5 alone, 10 together)

Gary Klein's method. Imagine it's a year from now. Your organization adopted your recommendation, and it failed. Write down every reason you can think of, for five minutes, without discussing them. Then a few of you read yours aloud, and we sort them together.

Sort each reason:

  • A limit of your evidence (a rival you couldn't rule out, a rung you couldn't reach) → it goes in the white paper's limits.
  • A condition where it's adopted (a support factor that may be missing, in Cartwright's terms) → it goes in your forecast's assumptions.
  • Something nobody could have known → leave it out, unless it would change the decision.

How the clinics work

Each clinic is about your own draft, worked at your seat. I set up the question, show one or two drafts on the screen with their authors' permission, and then you work while I come round to talk with anyone who wants to. The Skeptic Gem is your second reader in every clinic. Swapping with whoever is beside you is welcome and never required.

White paper clinic (40 minutes)

  1. The first paragraph. After reading it, could a reader say what decision is being made and what you recommend? If not, rewrite it so they could.
  2. The claim. Write it in one sentence. Is it the same sentence in the paper, on the slides, and in the talk you will record?
  3. The rung. Name the rung of Pearl's ladder your evidence reaches, and check that no verb in the paper climbs higher than that.
  4. The diagram. Is it in the paper, with the rival you took most seriously?

Then, as the decision-maker, finish the sentence "On Monday, I would… because…". If the sentence you get back isn't the recommendation you meant, the paper isn't done.

Forecast clinic (40 minutes)

  1. Write the forecast sentence. "If the organization adopts this, we expect … between … and … over …, assuming …". A range, a time, and the main assumption.
  2. List the support factors (Cartwright). What has to be present where it is adopted for it to work there? Every "not sure" is an assumption the sentence has to state.
  3. For whom or when might it not hold? Name the condition, with a reason you held before looking at the data.
  4. Ask what would make it wrong. Give the Skeptic Gem your sentence and ask what would put the outcome outside your range. Widen the range, or name the condition, so a decision-maker knows what to watch.

Last, check the range against the rung. A forecast that is more certain than your evidence is the most common way a careful paper overclaims.

Chart and slide clinic (35 minutes)

  1. Every chart says what question it answers. If you can't finish "This chart answers …", cut the chart.
  2. Rates, levels and counts. Divide by the right denominator, name the level of the data, and show the count behind every average.
  3. Where your biggest limit sits. Open your slides. If the limitation that matters most is the last bullet of a late slide, move it.
  4. One argument. The same numbers and the same claim in the paper and on the slides.

Your recorded run-through (due with the pre-final)

The run-through happens once everything is complete, so it is recorded, not given in class. Record your whole talk, 10 minutes (Google Meet or Google Slides recording works), upload it to Google Drive with your Willamette account, and post the link to your pod's thread with your pre-final version by Thu Dec 3, 6:00 pm. It earns the participation point for the run-through, and it can be the first draft of your final video. Your pod watches it in Thursday's review. Before you record, answer these four questions about your own talk.

  1. What is the claim, in one sentence? Does the talk say it?
  2. Which rung does the evidence reach? Does the talk say so?
  3. What would it take to know more, and is it worth it?
  4. What's the forecast, as a range, and what assumption does it rest on?

Your fix list (25 minutes)

From the premortem and the three clinics, write the three changes you'll make before Thursday's pre-final version. Start with whatever would make a reader misread your claim. Then set a time to record your run-through. Post the list to your pod's thread, and your pod will look for those changes on Thursday.
