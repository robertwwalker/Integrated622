Read before this session

This guide is also on the Module 3 readings page, with Thursday's other two readings and the full sequence of what's due.

Hubbard, How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of "Intangibles" in Business, 3rd ed. (Wiley, 2014), chapter 7, "Quantifying the Value of Information."

CoreLibrary ebook (365-day loan)About 50 minutes, with the guide

Why this reading. Tonight asks what would move you up Pearl's ladder. Chapter 7 asks the manager's next question: is it worth the climb? More data costs money and time, and it only has value if it might change a decision. Your white paper has to say what it would take to know more; this chapter turns that from a wish into a judgment. It finishes the Hubbard thread from Module 1: chapter 3 (anything that matters can be measured), chapter 4 (define the decision first), chapter 6 (ranges, not points). Skip the arithmetic; the ideas carry.

Before

The decision and the doubt (5 minutes)

Write the decision your white paper will recommend, in one sentence. Then name the one uncertainty that, if it were resolved, would be most likely to change that recommendation.

While

Find these

  1. The McNamara fallacy. The chapter opens with it. State its steps in your own words. Which step is most tempting in your project?
  2. The chance of being wrong and the cost of being wrong. Hubbard combines them into the expected opportunity loss. Say in words what it is. For your decision, what would being wrong cost, and who would pay?
  3. The measurement inversion. Organizations tend to measure what is easy, not what is most uncertain and most consequential. Which variable in your project is easiest to measure, and which matters most? Are they the same?
  4. Partial uncertainty reduction. Why does Hubbard say that a little information is often worth the most? What does that mean for a pilot like SmartShift at three sites?
  5. The "epiphany." What does he say changes once an organization starts asking what information is worth?
After

What it would take to know more (10 minutes)

Draft the sentence your white paper needs: the one further measurement that would most improve your recommendation, roughly what it would cost, and whether it is worth it given what being wrong would cost. If the answer is "not worth it," say so; that is a finding too. The loop never closes completely (see Projects at the boundary); this is how you decide which gap is worth closing next.
