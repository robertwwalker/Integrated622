DAGitty in ten minutes

DAGitty draws causal diagrams and reads them back to you: given your boxes and arrows, it says which comparisons can estimate the effect you care about. It knows only what you draw. The arrows are your claims, not its findings.

You can draw by clicking on the canvas (Huntington-Klein's two-page cheat sheet shows how), or paste a model. Here is SmartShift's diagram as a model. In DAGitty, open the "Model code" panel, replace what's there with this, and click "Update DAG":

dag { SmartShift [exposure] left_org [outcome] readiness [latent] readiness -> SmartShift readiness -> engagement readiness -> left_org site_type -> SmartShift site_type -> left_org SmartShift -> overtime overtime -> engagement overtime -> left_org engagement -> left_org engagement -> answered_survey HR_system -> left_org }

What does DAGitty say about estimating SmartShift's effect on leaving?

What we found
  • No comparison of measured columns closes the back door. Readiness is marked latent, meaning unmeasured, and it causes both SmartShift and leaving. Comparing within site type closes one back door, but not that one. That's why Tuesday's move matters: comparing changes removes readiness, if readiness stayed the same between 2024 and 2025.
  • Overtime and engagement are on the path from SmartShift to leaving. DAGitty won't tell you to compare within them, because they're how SmartShift might work. Comparing within overtime would remove part of the effect you want.
  • The HR system affects what got recorded as leaving, but it doesn't touch SmartShift, so it isn't a back door. It's a shock to the outcome, which a comparison of changes subtracts out if it hit both groups alike.
  • Delete the readiness box and DAGitty says to compare within site type. That's the diagram the data alone would let you believe.

To keep a diagram, take a screenshot, or copy the model code into your notes. Saving to dagitty.net itself needs an internet connection.

Reading a diagram back

DAGitty gives you three things once you have drawn a diagram. Paths, every route between the cause and the outcome, some carrying the effect and some carrying bias. An adjustment set, what to compare within, if anything works. And testable implications, things that must be true in the data if the diagram is right. The first two tell you what to do. The third tells you whether to believe the diagram. Here is what DAGitty says about SmartShift's diagram (the model code above), computed with DAGitty itself.

Seven paths from SmartShift to leaving

PathKind
1SmartShift → overtime → leavingCausal
2SmartShift → overtime → engagement → leavingCausal
3SmartShift ← readiness → leavingBack door, open
4SmartShift ← readiness → engagement → leavingBack door, open
5SmartShift ← site type → leavingBack door, open
6SmartShift → overtime → engagement ← readiness → leavingBlocked at a collider (engagement)
7SmartShift ← readiness → engagement ← overtime → leavingBlocked at a collider (engagement)

A causal path starts with an arrow out of SmartShift. A back door starts with an arrow into it.

What to compare within

With readiness unmeasured, DAGitty says no adjustment set exists: no comparison of measured variables will do, because the back door through readiness stays open. If readiness had been measured, the answer would be to compare within readiness and site type. For the direct effect the set would add overtime, the mediator. You would not do that for the total effect, because adjusting for the road the effect travels removes the effect.

A mediator and a collider

A mediator is the road the effect travels (SmartShift → overtime → leaving); comparing within it removes part of the effect. A collider is where two arrows meet (HR system → leaving ← overtime); comparing within it creates a link that was not there. DAGitty confirms it: the HR system change and overtime are unrelated until you look only at the people who left, and then they are connected. Looking only at people who answered a survey, or only at people who left, is looking within a collider or its child.

What must be true if the diagram is right

DAGitty lists ten testable implications for this diagram. Three of them: SmartShift and the HR system change are unrelated (the HR change hit every site, whatever it got), site type and overtime are unrelated once you know whether a site got SmartShift, and overtime and whether someone answered the survey are unrelated once you know their engagement. If one fails in the data, the diagram is wrong.

T1

Test one implication

Using staff_quarters, take only 2024, before SmartShift started anywhere, and leave out site S6's rows for 2024Q3 and 2024Q4. Compare the average overtime_hours by site type: urban (S1, S2), suburban (S3, S4, S5) and rural (S6, S7, S8), with the number of rows in each. Then tell me whether site type is related to overtime, and what that would mean for a causal diagram in which site type affects who got SmartShift and affects leaving, but is drawn with no arrow into overtime.

The diagram says site type does not touch overtime once you know who got SmartShift. Does the data agree?

What we found
Site typeAverage overtime, 2024Rows
Rural (S6, S7, S8)29.9 hours414
Suburban (S3, S4, S5)38.5590

It fails. Every rural site is below every urban and suburban site, so site type is related to overtime and the diagram is missing an arrow from site type to overtime. Adding it does not change what to adjust for (site type was already in the set), but it changes the story: site type is a cause of overtime, of who got SmartShift, and of leaving. The habit: draw it, read it back, test one thing it says must be true, and redraw. Gemini's numbers should match; watch its vocabulary, which may borrow terms (such as "exclusion restriction") that do not describe this.

Fernhill: the missing box (15 minutes)

Fernhill's version 2 ran a fair trial, and its Results draft ends by saying what it cannot settle: the new structure delivers less on hard work, and the diagram has no box for what happens between the lead, who judges the technical path, and the manager, who sets priorities. Here is version 2's diagram as a model. In DAGitty, open the "Model code" panel, replace what's there with this, and click "Update DAG":

dag { arm [exposure] delivered_share [outcome] arm -> manager_type arm -> ai_access manager_type -> esc_wait_hrs manager_type -> delivered_share ai_access -> escalations escalations -> esc_wait_hrs hard_plan -> escalations esc_wait_hrs -> delivered_share }
F1

Read it back

What does DAGitty say about estimating the arm's effect on delivery? Why does assignment by lot make that answer so simple?

What we found

Nothing needs adjusting. The lot has no arrows into it, so no back door runs through it and a direct comparison of arms estimates the effect of the arm. That is what randomizing buys. It does not buy an explanation: the diagram says nothing about why hard work delivers less in the new structure.

Ask of each box: what shapes it? what does it change? does it carry over from one quarter to the next? could Fernhill measure it, and when would it have had to start?

Does Tuesday's comparison hold up?

On Tuesday, comparing changes gave SmartShift about 12 fewer overtime hours a quarter and about 4.2 points less leaving, relative to the other sites. Each of these demos tests one thing that comparison assumes. Paste each prompt into your Riverbend chat. Think about the question in bold before you open "What we found." Gemini's wording will differ from ours, but the numbers should match.

D1

Were they parallel before?

Using staff_quarters, for each quarter show (a) the average overtime_hours, leaving out site S6's rows for 2024Q3 and 2024Q4, and (b) the share of rows with left_org = 1, using every row. Do both separately for the SmartShift sites (S1, S4, S7) and the other five, and chart both as lines by quarter. Then pretend SmartShift started in mid-2024: compare the change from the first half of 2024 to the second half, for the two groups, for both measures.

Overtime ran in parallel through 2024, about 5 hours apart, then the SmartShift sites dropped 13 hours in 2025Q1 and stayed there. The mid-2024 "fake start" gives almost nothing (−0.2 hours). Leaving is noisier: quarters swing by 5 to 7 points at the SmartShift sites, which have about 160 staff, and the fake start gives +2.1 points. Parallel enough to believe the overtime comparison; leaving needs more care.

Notice 2025Q1 in the "others" column: leaving dipped at those sites too. That's the HR system change, and the comparison subtracts it out.

D2

The broken batch, one more time

Repeat the comparison of changes for overtime_hours (the SmartShift sites' change from 2024 to 2025, minus the other sites' change), but this time keep every row, including S6's 2024Q3 and 2024Q4. Then do it once more using medians instead of averages.

Which way does the answer move?

What we found

With S6's broken quarters in, the other sites' 2024 average jumps to 75.4 hours, so their "fall" to 36.2 looks enormous. The difference in changes flips from −12.1 to +25.5 hours: SmartShift would appear to raise overtime. Medians are steadier, about −10.5. A causal estimate inherits every problem in the data underneath it. Module 1's audit wasn't a warm-up.

D3

How sure? Placebo sites

There are 56 ways to pick three of Riverbend's eight sites. For every one of them, pretend those three sites got SmartShift and compute the same comparison of changes, for overtime (leaving out S6's 2024Q3 and 2024Q4) and for the share leaving (using every row). Where do S1, S4, and S7 rank among the 56? Show the range.

What would you tell the board about each outcome?

What we found
  • Overtime: the real three rank 1st of 56, the largest drop of any trio. If SmartShift did nothing, a first-place finish would be about a 1-in-56 chance.
  • Leaving: they rank 6th of 56. Trios range from about −6 to +6 points, so −4.2 is near the edge, but five made-up trios did as well or better.

For the board: the overtime evidence is strong. The turnover evidence is suggestive and consistent with a real effect, but eight sites can't rule out chance. This is how sure you can be, without a formula.

D4

Same people, or different people?

Among staff who answered the engagement question at least once in 2024 and at least once in 2025, compare each person's average engagement in 2025 with their average in 2024. Report the average change for people at SmartShift sites and at the other sites, with the number of people in each.

In Module 2, more people started answering the survey at SmartShift sites. Does the rise in engagement survive looking at the same people?

What we found

Grouping people by their 2024 site, the same people's engagement rose 0.18 at SmartShift sites (131 people) and 0.02 at the others (163 people). Grouping them by their 2025 site gives 0.21 and −0.01. The rise isn't just a different group answering. Comparing the same people over time is a comparison of changes too, one person at a time. But 23 people changed sites, so say which rule you used, and notice that Gemini may pick a different one.

D5

One effect, three estimates

Repeat the comparison of changes for overtime (leaving out S6's 2024Q3 and 2024Q4) and for the share leaving (using every row), separately within each site type: S1 against S2 (urban), S4 against S3 and S5 (suburban), and S7 against S6 and S8 (rural).

What would you report?

Overtime falls in every comparison. Leaving falls a lot in two and not at all in the third, where S3, without SmartShift, also improved. Each comparison rests on one or two sites. Report the range, not the most flattering number.

What would move you up the ladder, and is it worth it?

Every design rests on something it can't check from inside. The higher the rung, the less it rests on.

DesignRungWhat has to be true
Compare adopters with everyone else1Nothing else differs between them. Rarely true: who chose?
Before and after, at the adopters only1Nothing else changed at the same time. At Riverbend, the HR system did.
Compare changes (difference-in-differences)toward 2The groups would have moved in parallel; nothing else changed at only one of them
Roll out in an order chosen by lottery2Willingness to let chance decide who goes first
A randomized experiment (Bloom's hybrid trial)2Chance decides who gets it; enough people; the same measures for both groups
What would have happened to this person3A causal model whose assumptions can't all be tested

Then Hubbard's question: is the climb worth it? For Riverbend, the five remaining sites will adopt SmartShift in some order anyway. Letting a lottery pick the order costs almost nothing, and within a year it would settle the turnover question that the eight-site comparison can't. That's Hubbard's "partial uncertainty reduction": a little information, where the uncertainty is greatest, is often worth the most.

Your next rung (you'll do this for your own project in W5, below)

One sentence each: the rung your evidence reaches now; the cheapest design that would get you one rung higher; what being wrong would cost, and who would pay; and whether it's worth it. These are sections 3 and 4 of your Interpretation.

Forecasting the effect of a decision

Leadership's real question is whether to roll SmartShift out to the other five sites. That needs an intervention forecast, the third kind on the Models page: if we act, what happens? It has four ingredients:

  1. The estimate and its range: Tuesday's comparison, with D3 and D5 telling you how wide to make it.
  2. The mechanism you believe: your diagram. SmartShift → overtime → leaving.
  3. Whether it travels: the three adopters were the readiest sites in their types. Will it work as well at the others? (Cartwright, before the dry run.)
  4. Ranges, not points, run forward: a Monte Carlo simulation, as in Hubbard, ch. 6, and Module 1.
Riverbend's other five sites have about 240 staff, and 134 of them left in 2025. Suppose rolling out SmartShift lowers each quarter's share leaving by somewhere between 0 and 8 points, most likely around 4. Simulate 10,000 rollouts and give me the number of departures avoided next year: the middle, and a 90 percent range. Then list every assumption you made.

How would you say the answer to leadership?

What we found

About 40 fewer departures a year in the middle, and roughly 10 to 65 in a 90 percent range. The range is wide because the estimate is uncertain, and it doesn't yet allow for the fact that the five sites are less ready. A good sentence: "If SmartShift is rolled out to the other five sites, we expect between about 10 and 65 fewer departures next year, assuming its effect there is similar to its effect at the three pilot sites, which were the readiest of their types."

Back to card 12 from Module 2's question sort. A leaving model, the umbrella, says which staff to check in with. This forecast, the rain dance, says whether SmartShift is worth rolling out. A real decision often needs both.

W2

W3

Write DAGitty's answer as a sentence: "To estimate the effect of [my change] on [my outcome], I would have to compare within …" If it says no comparison of measured variables will do, name the unobserved box that blocks it, and the design that would get around it: a comparison of changes? an experiment?

The Interpretation section

Copy the outline into your own document and fill it in as you go. Tonight's pod post is sections 1, 3, 4, and 5.

INTERPRETATION 1. My causal claim In words that claim exactly what the evidence supports (Hernán). 2. The rung my evidence reaches Seeing, doing, or imagining, and the design that got me there: a comparison of levels, before and after, a comparison of changes, an experiment, or a simulation from a stated model. How many comparisons or cuts I tried to get here, counted in my own chat, and which one I am reporting. 4. What it would take to know more The next step up the ladder, roughly what it would cost, and whether it's worth it, given what being wrong would cost (Hubbard). 5. My forecast for the decision If [decision] is adopted, I expect [outcome] to be between [low] and [high] over [time], assuming [the main assumptions, including whether it travels to where it's adopted].

The white paper (2–4 pages)

Written for a decision-maker in your organization, not for me. A suggested order:

  1. The decision and your recommendation, in the first paragraph.
  2. The question and the model (your Brief).
  3. The data, and how it was gathered or simulated (Data & Methods). Say plainly if it's simulated.
  4. What the data shows: one or two charts, each with "This chart answers: …" (Results).
  5. What it means: your claim, its rung, and your diagram (Interpretation).
  6. The forecast: a range, and what it assumes.
  7. What it would take to know more.
  8. A note on AI: what your AI analyst did, and how you checked it.

The slides and the video

About 8 to 10 slides for a talk of the length we agree in class: no more than 15 minutes, and probably about 10. A suggested arc: the decision; the model, as a diagram; the data; the key chart; the claim and its rung; the strongest rival; the forecast's range; what would settle it; the recommendation. Put your biggest limitation in the middle, not in the last bullet of the last slide.
