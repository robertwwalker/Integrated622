Debrief: the Module 2 exercise

Two parts of the exercise lead straight into tonight.

Part 5: a list for the start of a quarter

The In-person 2 model picked the leaver over a stayer about 7 times in 10, using what was measured in the quarter people left. Using only last quarter's information, which is all a start-of-quarter list would have, it drops to about 65 in 100, and about 60 on 2025, a year it never saw. Still better than a coin toss, and still an umbrella, not a rain dance.

The training gap told the same story. In the same quarter, 29% of leavers' rows completed training against 40% of stayers'. Using last quarter's training, it's 38% against 40%. Most of the gap was timing: people who leave during a quarter often don't finish that quarter's training. Hold on to that; we come back to training at 7:05 with a second explanation.

Part 6: SmartShift and overtime

Overtime fell at all three SmartShift sites in 2025, from about 33 to about 20 hours a quarter, and barely moved elsewhere, once S6's broken quarters are out. With them in, the other sites appear to fall further, and SmartShift looks worse than nothing. The best answers named what we'd need to know: who chose the three sites, whether the HR system change affected the records, and a comparison of changes rather than levels. Tonight answers the first and the last.

Why correlation isn't enough: Pearl's ladder

You met the ladder in Projects at the boundary. Here it is again, with the question each rung asks of Riverbend.

RungThe questionAt RiverbendWhat can answer it
1. Seeing
association		What goes with what?Do SmartShift sites have lower turnover?Data and pattern-finding: statistics, machine learning, AI
2. Doing
intervention		What happens if we act?If we roll SmartShift out everywhere, will turnover fall?An experiment, or data plus a causal model you're willing to defend
3. Imagining
counterfactuals		What would have happened otherwise?Would this nurse have stayed if their site had never adopted SmartShift?A causal model, resting on assumptions that can't all be tested

The rule from the reading: no amount of first-rung data, by itself, answers a second-rung question. Every rung above the first needs something the data can't supply. That's the answer to Anderson from Module 2: "The End of Theory" is a first-rung claim about a second-rung world.

Activity: the ladder sort (15 minutes, the whole room)

Put each question on a rung: 1 seeing, 2 doing, or 3 imagining. One card is a trap. In the room each question comes up on the screen and the room shows a hand for 1, 2, or 3; on your own, work in a document.

Nonprofit

  1. Which donors gave more after the gala?
  2. Would a matching-gift campaign raise more?
  3. Would this volunteer have stayed if we'd assigned them a mentor?
  4. Do clients with case managers miss fewer appointments?

Public

  1. Did response times change after the new dispatch system went live?
  2. If we add a second inspector, will overdue inspections fall?
  3. Would this applicant have been approved under the old rule?
  4. Do districts with more code officers have fewer violations?

Private

  1. Do app users spend more?
  2. If we give everyone the app discount, will spending rise?
  3. Would this customer have churned without the retention call?
  4. We pay bonuses on our customer satisfaction score. Will raising the score raise retention?
Check your sort (after you've tried)
  • Rung 1, seeing: 1, 4, 5, 8, 9. Card 5 sounds like a before-and-after, but a change over time is still an association: something else may have changed at the same time.
  • Rung 2, doing: 2, 6, 10, 12.
  • Rung 3, imagining: 3, 7, 11. Each asks about one person, in a world that didn't happen.
  • The trap is 12. It's Goodhart's causal variant, from Module 1's optional reading: the score may move with retention without causing it. Push on the score (through bonuses) and staff may raise the score without raising retention.

Cards 1, 4, and 9 are also about who chose to be in the group. Hold on to that for 8:25.

Causal diagrams: three shapes

A causal diagram is your model in boxes and arrows. An arrow is a claim. A missing arrow is a stronger claim: it says one thing doesn't affect the other at all. Almost every diagram is built from three shapes.

ShapeWhat it looks likeAt RiverbendWhat it means for a comparison
Chain
a mediator		A → M → BSmartShift → overtime → leavingOvertime is how SmartShift might work. Don't compare within overtime levels if you want SmartShift's whole effect; you'd remove part of it.
Fork
a confounder		A ← C → BA well-run, engaged team → more training, and → fewer departuresThe common cause makes A and B move together even if A does nothing. Compare within it, if you can measure it.
ColliderA → C ← BEngaged → answers the survey ← stays longerLooking only at people who answered can create a pattern that isn't there. Don't compare within a collider.

The words "confounder," "mediator," and "collider" are in the readings. The pictures matter more than the names: draw first, then decide what to compare.

Worked example: does training keep people?

At Module 2's In-person 2, staff who left had completed training less often. Overall, 13.6% of rows without training lead to a departure, against 9.0% with it (1,975 and 1,240 rows). The Module 2 exercise found one explanation: timing. Here's a second. Draw the fork: what makes someone both likely to train and likely to stay? Engagement is a candidate. Training rises steeply with it: 19% of rows at engagement 1 completed training, and 70% at engagement 5. Now compare within each engagement answer:

"I would recommend Riverbend as a place to work"12345
Leaving, no training that quarter28.9% (128)13.4% (322)9.0% (435)5.4% (278)3.8% (53)
Leaving, completed training22.6% (31)14.3% (105)9.4% (278)5.2% (347)2.5% (122)

At answers 2, 3, and 4, the gap is gone. At 1 and 5 the groups are small. The engaged train and stay; training itself shows little sign of keeping anyone. Two rivals, timing and a common cause, both supported, and neither is "training keeps people." We drew the fork first and checked it second. That's the order.

Activity: draw it (25 minutes, at your seat, after dinner)

For each story: draw the diagram, name the confounder, and say what comparison would remove it. Paper first, at your seat. Talk it over with whoever is beside you if you like, and nobody has to; then we draw each one together on the screen.

Nonprofit

Students in an after-school program graduate at 82%, against 74% across the district. Families sign up for the program themselves. The board wants to expand it.

Public

A county's new permit software went first to the district whose manager asked for it. Six months later, that district issues permits 20% faster than the others.

Private

Customers who use the store's app spend 30% more. Marketing wants to pay customers to download it.

Some answers (after you've tried)
  • After-school program. Family engagement → enrolls in the program, and → graduates. Compare students with similar grades and schools before they enrolled, or their change in attendance or grades after enrolling against similar students who didn't.
  • Permit software. A motivated manager → asks for the software, and → runs a faster office anyway. Compare each district's change in permit times, before and after, not the levels. Better: roll it out to the remaining districts in an order chosen by lottery.
  • The app. Being a big spender → downloads the app, and → spends more. Compare customers' spending before and after they downloaded it, against similar customers who didn't. Paying people to download won't make them big spenders.

Every one of these is Module 2's "who chose to be in the group?" Each answer ends in the same move: compare changes, or decide who gets it by chance. That's next.

Who chose S1, S4, and S7?

In Module 1, Projects at the boundary asked who chose the three SmartShift sites, and why, and promised we'd come back to it. Riverbend's records don't say. But one site of each type got SmartShift, so compare each with the others of its type, in 2024, before SmartShift existed.

In each type, SmartShift went to the site whose staff were most engaged the year before. Whoever chose seems to have picked the readiest site in each type. That's a pattern, not a record of the decision; Riverbend's data doesn't say who decided or why. (S7 is a reminder that the patterns aren't neat: the most engaged rural site, with the highest rural turnover.)

Draw it: readiness → SmartShift, and readiness → leaving. That's a fork, and it's why Module 2's comparison of levels can't tell us what SmartShift did. Readiness isn't a column in the data, so we can't compare within it. We need another move.

Compare changes, not levels

If readiness is a difference between sites that stays the same from year to year, it's in both years. So compare each group's change from 2024 to 2025, and then subtract one change from the other. The other sites' change stands in for what would have happened at the SmartShift sites without SmartShift. That's John Snow's move from the reading, in 1854. Economists call it difference-in-differences.

What it needs: the two groups would have moved in parallel without the change. Nothing else can have changed at only one group's sites.

Activity: fill the 2×2 (10 minutes, at your seat, paper)

For each card: the change in the group that got it, the change in the group that didn't, and the difference. Then: what would break it?

Public

A new dispatch system went live in District A, not District B. Median response time: A went from 9.0 to 7.5 minutes; B from 8.0 to 7.6.

Nonprofit

New volunteer onboarding at the north site, not the south. Still active after 90 days: north 60% → 72%; south 55% → 61%.

Private

A scheduling app in the West region, not the East. Overtime hours per worker per month: West 22 → 15; East 18 → 17. But East had been falling 2 hours a year, and West rising 2.

Check your 2×2 (after you've tried)
  • Dispatch: A fell 1.5 minutes, B fell 0.4. The difference in changes is about 1.1 minutes faster. Breaks if A also changed how "response time" is recorded (redefined), or hired dispatchers at the same time.
  • Onboarding: north rose 12 points, south 6. The difference is about 6 points. Breaks if the north site also got a new volunteer coordinator, or if the volunteers who joined that year were a different kind of person.
  • Scheduling app: West fell 7, East fell 1, so the difference is 6 hours. But the trends weren't parallel. On its trend, West should have risen to about 24, so it fell about 9 against it; East should have fallen to about 16, so it rose about 1 against it. That's a difference of about 10, not 6. The plain 2×2 misleads until you deal with the trends. Always look at the "before" years first.

Two questions, first alone and then together:

  1. The HR system. Riverbend-wide, recorded departures fell from 13.1% in 2024Q4 to 8.0% in 2025Q1, at nearly every site, when the new HR system went live. What does subtracting the other sites' change do to that dip? What would a before-and-after at the SmartShift sites alone have credited to SmartShift?
  2. Is −4.2 points "the effect of SmartShift"? Name what it still assumes.
What we'd say (after you've tried)
  1. If the HR system's dip hit both groups equally, subtracting the other sites' change removes it. A before-and-after at S1, S4, and S7 alone would have counted it as SmartShift's.
  2. It assumes the two groups would have moved in parallel without SmartShift; that nothing else changed only at the SmartShift sites; and that readiness, the reason they were chosen, didn't itself change between the years. And three sites against five is a small comparison. Thursday tests each of these.

On the ladder, a comparison of changes reaches toward the second rung. How far depends on the assumptions, and the honest answer says so.
