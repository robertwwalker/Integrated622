Module 3 · Readings

Module 3 readings

About two hours of reading before each of the first two sessions, and a lighter set for the dry run, with an active reading guide for every reading.

How to use these guides

The same three-part guide as Modules 1 and 2: before you read, write a prediction or a question from your own project; while you read, find four or five things and answer each in your own words; after you read, write a short piece for your project. In Module 3, every "after" feeds the Interpretation section of your white paper, or the white paper and slides as a whole. Each guide also says which part of the Module 3 exercise it prepares you for. Keep your answers in your reading notebook.

One book runs through the module, as Hubbard ran through Module 1 and Peng and Matsui through Module 2: Nick Huntington-Klein, The Effect: An Introduction to Research Design and Causality (2021), free online. Three of its chapters take you from what a causal diagram is, to how to draw your own, to comparing changes instead of levels. Skip any code and any equations; the argument is in the prose and the pictures.

Read ahead. Everything is posted now. Module 3 is short: the kick-off and Online 1 are two days apart for Salem and one day apart for Portland, and the exercise is due that Sunday. Do the Online 1 readings before your in-person night if you can.

Unfamiliar references: keep reading with your AI analyst

These readings assume things you haven't met, too. Mark them, ask Gemini about the ones that matter, and check one of its answers against a second source.

I'm reading [title and author] for an MBA course on data and decision science. I ran into this reference and don't know it: [the name, model, or term, with the sentence it appears in]. Explain what it is in plain language, why the author might mention it here, and give me one source I could check your answer against.

Open readings are free online. Library readings are through the Willamette University Library; Canvas shows where. Each session page also offers a few optional podcasts or videos under "Optional: watch or listen."

Read before the Module 3 kick-off · Tue Nov 17 (Salem) · Wed Nov 18 (Portland)

Why seeing isn't doing, and comparing changes

Module 2 stayed on the first rung of Pearl's ladder: what goes with what. Your Results draft says what your data shows, and Hernán's rule kept you from claiming more. Module 3 climbs. The first reading is the ladder, from the person who built it. The second starts the book that runs through the module: your model as boxes and arrows. The third is the move Module 2 ended on: "What if you compared their changes?"

1. Pearl and Mackenzie: the ladder of causation

Judea Pearl and Dana Mackenzie, The Book of Why: The New Science of Cause and Effect (Basic Books, 2018), chapter 1, "The Ladder of Causation." Free from the authors' site.

CoreOpenAbout 45 minutesPrepares: every part's rung

Why this reading. You met the ladder as a preview in Projects at the boundary: seeing, doing, imagining. This is the full account. Every conclusion you write in Module 3, in the exercise and in your white paper, has to say which rung it reaches.

Before

Which rung? (3 minutes)

Which rung is your Results draft on? Which rung does your white paper's recommendation need?

While

Find these

  1. The three rungs. What question belongs on each, and which creature or machine does he put there?
  2. The rooster and the sunrise. Why can't data on the first rung, however much of it, tell you that the rooster doesn't cause the sunrise?
  3. Machine learning. Where on the ladder does he put it, and why? Compare Module 2's umbrella and rain dance.
  4. "Do." In plain words, what is the difference between seeing that something happened and making it happen?
  5. An old argument. Is this chapter a reply to Anderson's "The End of Theory" from Module 2? Where would Box agree with Pearl?
After

Your question on three rungs (10 minutes)

Rewrite your Project Brief's question (element 3) three times, once for each rung. Which version does your data reach now? Which version does your decision-maker need answered?

2. The Effect: causal diagrams

Nick Huntington-Klein, The Effect: An Introduction to Research Design and Causality (CRC Press, 2021), chapter 6, "Causal Diagrams." Free online.

CoreOpenAbout 30 minutesPrepares: exercise Parts 1 and 2

Why this reading. On the Models page, the third step up the ladder of explicitness is a diagram: boxes and arrows. In Module 3 it becomes a causal diagram, a statement of your model that others can see and argue with, and that tells you which comparisons your data can and can't make.

Before

Your model in boxes (5 minutes)

Without reading anything, sketch your Project Brief's model (element 2) as boxes and arrows. Keep it; you'll revise it.

While

Find these

  1. Nodes and arrows. What does each stand for? What is a "data generating process"?
  2. The missing arrow. What does it claim when two boxes are not connected? Is that a weaker claim than an arrow, or a stronger one?
  3. Simple or complete? How does he decide what to leave off a diagram? Compare Box on parsimony from Module 1.
  4. One of his examples, redrawn. Pick one of his diagrams and redraw it for Riverbend or for your own organization.
After

Add your rival (10 minutes)

Redraw your sketch using his rules. Then add the strongest rival explanation from section 5 of your Results draft as boxes and arrows. Bring it to the kick-off on paper: it's your first turn as Author in tonight's Skeptic's Chair.

3. The Effect: difference-in-differences

Huntington-Klein, The Effect, chapter 18, "Difference-in-Differences." Read from the start through the section "Difference-in-Differences and Dirty Water," on John Snow and the London water companies of 1854.

CoreOpenAbout 35 minutesPrepares: exercise Part 3

Why this reading. Module 2's last session ended on a hint about SmartShift: the three sites were different before it arrived, so comparing their levels can't tell you what it did. "What if you compared their changes?" This chapter is that move, and its founding example is a natural experiment from 1854.

Before

Your guess (3 minutes)

In one sentence: if you compared how much the SmartShift sites changed from 2024 to 2025 with how much the other sites changed, what do you expect you'd find, and what would that comparison remove?

While

Find these

  1. Within and between. What does he mean by variation within a group over time, and variation between groups? Which one does the method use?
  2. The comparison group. What does the group that didn't change stand in for?
  3. Parallel trends. In your own words, what has to be true for the method to work? Can the data before the change check it?
  4. Snow's water. Why do the two water companies make a good comparison? What would have broken it?
After

Your comparison group (10 minutes)

For your own project: what change are you evaluating, what group didn't get it, and what "before" period could show whether the two were moving in parallel? If no such group exists, say so; that's a limit your white paper will need to state.

Optional: Pearl on Simpson's paradox (about 30 minutes)

Judea Pearl, "Understanding Simpson's Paradox," The American Statistician 68 (2014): 8–13 (UCLA technical report R-414). Module 2's Berkeley admissions case asked which number to believe, the overall rate or the department rates. Pearl's answer: it depends on the causal diagram, not the data. Which of his diagrams fits Berkeley? Skip the notation.

Optional: Pearl's seven tools (about 30 minutes)

Judea Pearl, "The Seven Tools of Causal Inference, with Reflections on Machine Learning," Communications of the ACM 62, no. 3 (2019): 54–60. In Module 1, Projects at the boundary asked which of his seven tools your project would need, and to keep the answer for Module 3. Has your answer changed?

Optional: natural experiments, Snow to the 2021 Nobel (about 40 minutes)

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, "Natural Experiments Help Answer Important Questions," the popular science background to the 2021 economics prize to David Card, Joshua Angrist, and Guido Imbens. Card and Krueger's minimum-wage study compares changes in two states, the same move as Snow's. Then, if you want the history: David A. Freedman, "From Association to Causation: Some Remarks on the History of Statistics" (Berkeley Statistics technical report 521), by the author of Module 2's reading on the ecological fallacy. What did Snow do that a correlation couldn't?

Read before Online 1 · Thu Nov 19

Drawing it properly, and whether the climb is worth it

Thursday has two halves. In the first, you build your own model in DAGitty, a free diagram tool, and see what it says you must compare. In the second, you ask what it would take to know more, whether that's worth it, and what to forecast. The first reading finishes the Hubbard thread from Module 1. The second is how to draw your diagram. The third is what climbing the ladder looks like when a company does it.

1. Hubbard: the value of information

Douglas W. Hubbard, How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of "Intangibles" in Business, 3rd ed. (Wiley, 2014), chapter 7, "Quantifying the Value of Information."

CoreLibrary ebook (365-day loan)About 50 minutes, with the guidePrepares: exercise Part 6

Why this reading. Thursday asks what would move you up Pearl's ladder. Chapter 7 asks the manager's next question: is it worth the climb? More data costs money and time, and it only has value if it might change a decision. Your white paper has to say what it would take to know more; this chapter turns that from a wish into a judgment. It finishes the Hubbard thread from Module 1: chapter 3 (anything that matters can be measured), chapter 4 (define the decision first), chapter 6 (ranges, not points). Skip the arithmetic; the ideas carry.

Before

The decision and the doubt (5 minutes)

Write the decision your white paper will recommend, in one sentence. Then name the one uncertainty that, if it were resolved, would be most likely to change that recommendation.

While

Find these

  1. The McNamara fallacy. The chapter opens with it. State its steps in your own words. Which step is most tempting in your project?
  2. The chance of being wrong and the cost of being wrong. Hubbard combines them into the expected opportunity loss. Say in words what it is. For your decision, what would being wrong cost, and who would pay?
  3. The measurement inversion. Organizations tend to measure what is easy, not what is most uncertain and most consequential. Which variable in your project is easiest to measure, and which matters most? Are they the same?
  4. Partial uncertainty reduction. Why does Hubbard say that a little information is often worth the most? What does that mean for a pilot like SmartShift at three sites?
  5. The "epiphany." What does he say changes once an organization starts asking what information is worth?
After

What it would take to know more (10 minutes)

Draft the sentence your white paper needs: the one further measurement that would most improve your recommendation, roughly what it would cost, and whether it is worth it given what being wrong would cost. If the answer is "not worth it," say so; that is a finding too. The loop never closes completely (see Projects at the boundary); this is how you decide which gap is worth closing next.

2. The Effect: drawing causal diagrams

Huntington-Klein, The Effect, chapter 7, "Drawing Causal Diagrams." Free online.

CoreOpenAbout 30 minutesPrepares: exercise Part 1, and your project's diagram

Why this reading. Thursday's workshop is drawing your own model in DAGitty, a free tool that runs in your browser with no account (on its home page, click "Launch DAGitty online in your browser"). This chapter is the method: start from the cause you care about and the outcome, add what affects them, then simplify without losing what matters.

Before

Count your boxes (3 minutes)

Look at the diagram you drew for the kick-off. Is any concept from your Data & Methods variable table missing from it? Is anything on it that you have no data for?

While

Find these

  1. His steps. List the steps he follows to draw a diagram from a research question.
  2. Leaving things out. When is it safe to leave a variable off? When is leaving it off itself a claim?
  3. Things you can't measure. Why do unobserved variables stay on the diagram even when you have no data on them?
  4. Your kick-off diagram. Which of his steps did you skip?
After

Redraw, and mark your data (10 minutes)

Redraw your diagram on paper following his steps. Mark the cause you're asking about and the outcome, and mark which boxes you have data for. Bring it Thursday; you'll build it in DAGitty, and it goes in your white paper.

3. Bloom, Han, and Liang: a schedule change, randomized

Nicholas Bloom, Ruobing Han, and James Liang, "Hybrid Working from Home Improves Retention without Damaging Performance," Nature 630 (2024): 920–925. Free on PubMed Central. Read the abstract, the introduction, the figure on quit rates, and the discussion; skim the methods.

CoreOpenAbout 30 minutesPrepares: exercise Part 6

Why this reading. A company wanted to know whether a change to how people schedule their work would keep them. Instead of comparing the teams that chose it with the teams that didn't, it decided by chance who got it, then measured quits and performance. That's Riverbend's SmartShift question, asked on the second rung of the ladder.

Before

Your prediction (3 minutes)

Two days a week working from home: would quits rise or fall? Would performance? Write your guess and how confident you are.

While

Find these

  1. Who got it. How did they decide who worked hybrid? Why does that matter more than anything else in the paper?
  2. Who it helped. For which groups did quits fall most? What does that suggest about why it worked?
  3. Performance. Why did they measure performance as carefully as quits? What would a manager have assumed without it?
  4. The limits. What can this experiment not tell another company? What can't it tell Riverbend?
After

The experiment version (10 minutes)

Write the experiment version of your project's question: who would get the change, who wouldn't, how you'd decide, and what you'd measure. Then Hubbard's question: is it worth running?

Optional, and recommended: closing back doors (about 30 minutes)

Huntington-Klein, The Effect, chapter 8, "Causal Paths and Closing Back Doors." The chapter that turns a diagram into a decision about what to compare. If you read one optional chapter in Module 3, read this one. What DAGitty means when it lists what you must "adjust for": which paths between cause and outcome are the effect you want, and which are back doors that make two things move together for other reasons.

Optional: confounders, mediators, and colliders (about 45 minutes)

Julia M. Rohrer, "Thinking Clearly About Correlations and Causation: Graphical Causal Models for Observational Data," Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science 1, no. 1 (2018): 27–42. Free on the publisher's site. Written for researchers who don't do math. Why "controlling for everything" can make an analysis worse: one sentence each on a confounder, a mediator, and a collider, with an example of each from your own project.

Optional: DAGitty, and a course on causal diagrams

Huntington-Klein's DAGitty cheat sheet: two pages on the tool you'll use Thursday. And Miguel Hernán's free online course, Causal Diagrams: Draw Your Assumptions Before Your Conclusions (HarvardX), by the author of Module 2's "The C-Word." Watch the first lesson only.

Will it work there, and how to say so

A lighter set, for Thanksgiving week. Your white paper ends with a recommendation and a forecast: if the organization adopts it, what will happen? The first reading asks whether evidence from one place carries to another. The other two are about saying what you found honestly, to someone who has to decide.

1. Cartwright: will this policy work for you?

Nancy Cartwright, "Will This Policy Work for You? Predicting Effectiveness Better: How Philosophy Helps," Philosophy of Science 79, no. 5 (2012): 973–989. The link is Durham University's repository, which has a free PDF of the published article; the journal's own page needs the library.

CoreOpenAbout 45 minutesPrepares: your white paper's forecast

Why this reading. The course opened with a philosophy reading on models and closes with one on evidence. "It worked there" isn't "it will work here." This is the step from a comparison of changes to a forecast for a decision, and it's why your forecast needs assumptions, not just a number.

While

Find these

  1. Two claims. How does she distinguish "it works somewhere" from "it will work for us"?
  2. Support factors. What are they? Why might a good experiment elsewhere still fail where you adopt it?
  3. The role of a model. Why does she say a forecast needs a causal model of your setting, not just good evidence from another?
After

Your support factors (10 minutes)

List what has to be present where your recommendation would be adopted for it to work there. Is each one present? Every "not sure" is an assumption your forecast must state.

2. Blastland and colleagues: five rules for evidence communication

Michael Blastland, Alexandra L. J. Freeman, Sander van der Linden, Theresa M. Marteau, and David Spiegelhalter, "Five Rules for Evidence Communication," Nature 587 (2020): 362–364. Free: Nature's PDF, or the University of Cambridge repository copy.

CoreOpenAbout 15 minutesPrepares: your white paper and slides

Why this reading. Your white paper is written for a decision-maker. The usual advice is to persuade: one message, a neat story. These authors argue the opposite. Inform, don't persuade.

After

Check your draft (10 minutes)

Hold your white paper draft up against each rule. For each, mark one sentence that follows it and one that doesn't, and rewrite the one that doesn't.

3. Tufte: the Columbia slide

Edward Tufte, "Columbia Accident Investigation Board: The Boeing PowerPoint Slide."

CoreOpenAbout 15 minutesPrepares: your slides and the dry run

Why this reading. In 2003, engineers told NASA what they knew about a foam strike on the shuttle Columbia in a slide. The most important caveat was buried in its last bullet. Honest accounting isn't only what you say; it's where you put it.

After

Where is your biggest limit? (5 minutes)

Find the slide where your project's most important limitation appears. If it's the last bullet of a late slide, move it. Bring the revised deck to the dry run.

Optional: a premortem (about 10 minutes)

Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem," Harvard Business Review 85, no. 9 (September 2007): 18–19. Through the library. Imagine it's a year from now and your recommendation failed. Why? We do this at the dry run; each reason you find belongs in your white paper's limits or your forecast's assumptions.

Optional: communicating uncertainty (about 40 minutes)

Baruch Fischhoff and Alex L. Davis, "Communicating Scientific Uncertainty," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 111, suppl. 4 (2014): 13664–13671. Which of their three kinds of decision is the one your white paper supports, and what does it say about how to state your range?

Optional: evidence-based management, and business experiments

For the organizational-management track: Jeffrey Pfeffer and Robert I. Sutton, "Evidence-Based Management," Harvard Business Review 84, no. 1 (January 2006): 62–74. For the operations track, and anyone whose organization could test a change before rolling it out: Ron Kohavi and Stefan Thomke, "The Surprising Power of Online Experiments," Harvard Business Review 95, no. 5 (September–October 2017). Both through the library. Which of their practices would your organization find hardest to adopt, and why?
