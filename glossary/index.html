Statistics glossary

The words this course uses, in plain language, with the pages where you meet them

47 termsPlain language

You do not need to memorize any of this. If a word on a page stops you, look it up here, then go back. Where a term has an example, it comes from an organization; most entries link to the page where the idea is used. If a word is missing, tell Robert and it will be added.

Describing one variable

Mean

The sum divided by the count. One extreme value drags it: nine gifts of $50 and one of $10,000 average $1,045, which describes nobody.

Where you meet it: In-person 2

Median

The middle value when sorted; half the cases fall above it. One huge gift does not move it (it stays $50 in the donor example).

Where you meet it: In-person 2 · Online 1

Spread: range and standard deviation

How far values sit from the middle. The range is highest minus lowest, set by two cases. The standard deviation is roughly the typical distance from the mean.

Where you meet it: In-person 2 · Online 2

Distribution

Every value a variable takes and how often: its shape. “What shape?” is the fifth of the five questions for describing a variable.

Where you meet it: In-person 2 · Readings

Histogram and box plot

Two pictures of a distribution. A histogram counts cases in intervals. A box plot shows the median, the middle half of the cases, and unusual points, and suits side-by-side groups.

Where you meet it: Module 2 kick-off · In-person 2

Outlier

A value far from the rest. Before deleting it, ask: an error, a different kind of case, or the most important case in the data?

Where you meet it: In-person 2

Proportion, rate, ratio

A proportion is a share of a whole (25%). A rate is a count per unit of time (share leaving per quarter). A ratio divides one quantity by another (cases per caseworker). Say which you mean.

Where you meet it: Online 1 · Module 1 kick-off

Percentage point

The difference between two percentages. Turnover falling from 10% to 5% is five percentage points, or a 50% decline. Confusing the two exaggerates or hides change.

See also: Proportion, rate, ratio

What a variable is

Variable

Anything that can differ across cases: a column in a table.

Where you meet it: Module 1 kick-off · In-person 2

Observation and observational unit

An observation is one row. The unit is what a row is about: a person in a quarter, a site in a week. Each kind of unit gets its own table, which is why Riverbend has two files.

Where you meet it: Readings · Riverbend

Labels and ordered labels

Textbooks say nominal and ordinal. Labels name categories with no order (department). Ordered labels have an order but unknown distances (satisfaction 1–5); a mean of them is a choice you must defend.

Where you meet it: Module 1 kick-off

Operationalization

Turning a concept into something measurable: “engagement” becomes a survey item scored 1–5. The choice is a judgment, and many problems start there.

Where you meet it: Module 1 kick-off · In-person 2

Goodhart’s law

When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure, because people work on the number instead of what it stood for.

Where you meet it: Readings · Projects

McNamara fallacy

Measuring what is easy, then treating what is hard to measure as unimportant, and finally as nonexistent.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings

Five ways data lies

Missing

Values are absent, and why matters: follow-up surveys returned only by clients who stayed describe the stayers.

Where you meet it: In-person 2

Mismeasured

What is recorded is not the concept: “meals served” that counts meals prepared.

Where you meet it: In-person 2

Redefined

The definition changed partway through: a new case system changes when a case counts as closed.

Where you meet it: In-person 2

Selected

Only some cases reach the data: success stories come only from graduates.

Where you meet it: In-person 2

Mistimed

Dates are shifted, backfilled or batched: sales pulled into the quarter to meet a target.

Where you meet it: In-person 2

Noise

Random variation unrelated to what you study. A site of 40 staff moves its quarterly turnover rate 2.5 points each time one person leaves.

Where you meet it: In-person 2 · Online 2

Sample

The cases you have, standing in for the cases you care about. Ask who is missing.

Where you meet it: In-person 2 · Online 1

Relating two variables

Association

Two variables move together in the data. It does not say why.

Where you meet it: Module 2 kick-off · Module 2 Online 2

Correlation

A number from −1 to +1 for the strength and direction of a straight-line association. 0.2 is faint, 0.8 is clear. Riverbend’s waits and satisfaction sit near −0.7: negative means the same picture, tilted the other way.

Where you meet it: Module 2 Online 1 · Module 2 readings

Confounder

A third factor that influences both the supposed cause and the outcome. Better managers may lead sites to adopt a program and to keep staff.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings

Simpson’s paradox

A comparison reverses when you split the data by a group: one side wins overall, the other wins within every department.

Where you meet it: Module 2 readings

Ecological fallacy

Reading a pattern across groups (sites, districts) as if it held for the individuals inside them.

Where you meet it: Module 2 readings · Module 2 Online 1

Mediator

A step between cause and effect: training builds skills, skills raise performance. Adjusting for a mediator removes the effect you wanted to see.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings

Collider

Something caused by two other things. If talent and connections both get people into a program, then among those admitted they look negatively related even if unrelated overall.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings

Cause and comparison

Causal claim

A statement about what would happen if something were changed. It needs an experiment, or data plus a causal model whose assumptions you state.

Where you meet it: Projects · Module 3 readings

Counterfactual

What would have happened otherwise. We never see it, so every causal claim rests on an argument about it.

Where you meet it: Projects

Ladder of causation

Pearl’s three rungs: seeing (what goes with what), doing (what happens if we change it), imagining (what would have happened otherwise). Say which rung your evidence reaches.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings

Experiment (randomized)

Chance decides who gets the change, so the groups differ only by the change. The cleanest route to a causal claim, and often unavailable in organizations.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings · Online 2

Natural experiment

A rule, lottery or accident outside the groups’ control decides who gets the change, so the comparison is close to an experiment.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings

Difference-in-differences

Compare how much the group that got the change moved with how much the other group moved, then subtract. A fixed gap between the groups cancels; only a gap in change remains.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings  |  See also: Parallel trends

Parallel trends

The assumption behind difference-in-differences: without the change, both groups would have moved together. Data from before the change can check it, not prove it.

Where you meet it: Module 3 readings

Regression to the mean

After an unusually high or low reading, the next tends to be nearer normal even if nothing was done. It matters when a program starts because of a bad stretch. Compare with groups chosen the same way.

See also: Noise · Difference-in-differences

Uncertainty and forecasting

Uncertainty

Data are incomplete or imprecise, to varying degrees. An honest answer says how sure we are; we may still have to decide, knowing the range.

Where you meet it: Home page · Models

Probability

Jaynes called it the logic of science: TRUE and FALSE extended to everything between, so evidence raises or lowers confidence in a model.

Where you meet it: Home page

Prediction and forecast

A prediction guesses an outcome for a case (who will leave?). A forecast looks ahead at a quantity, ideally with a range. A good prediction need not explain anything.

Where you meet it: Module 2 In-person 2 · Models

AUC

Pick one person who left and one who stayed: the AUC is the chance the model scored the leaver higher. 0.5 is a coin toss; 1.0 is perfect.

Where you meet it: Module 2 In-person 2

Simulation

Fake data made from a model you state, to see what real data would look like if the model were true. You specify; the AI generates; you check it did what you said.

Where you meet it: In-person 2 · Online 2

Monte Carlo

Repeating a simulation many times with random draws, to see the range of outcomes rather than one guess.

Where you meet it: Readings  |  See also: Simulation

Expected opportunity loss

The chance of being wrong times the cost of being wrong (Hubbard). Measure next whatever would most change the decision.

Where you meet it: Module 3 Online 1 · Module 3 readings

Data and process

Data dictionary

What every column is, how it was measured, and what its values mean. Check the AI’s counts and definitions against it.

Where you meet it: Riverbend dictionary · Online 1

Join

Combining two tables through a shared key. A careless join duplicates rows and quietly inflates counts.

Where you meet it: Online 1 · SQL

Panel data

The same units followed over time, such as the same staff each quarter, so you can compare a unit with itself.

Where you meet it: Readings

Little’s Law

In a steady process, the average number in the system equals the arrival rate times the average time each stays.
