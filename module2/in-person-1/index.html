Debrief: look before you summarize

In the Module 1 exercise you audited an AI's audit of Riverbend. One thing it should have flagged: at site S6, in 2024Q3 and 2024Q4, overtime_hours jumps from about 30 a quarter to about 470, then drops back. It looks like total hours exported into the overtime column. That's mismeasured, one of the five ways data lies.

Tonight's question is what those 84 rows do to an analysis that relates two variables.

All rowsWithout S6's two quarters
Mean overtime per quarter44.1 hours32.6 hours
Median overtime per quarter31.8 hours31.1 hours
Correlation: overtime and engagement−0.12−0.44
Correlation: overtime and leaving0.040.21

The median barely moved; the mean and both correlations moved a lot. With the bad rows in, the correlations say "almost no relationship." Without them, the more overtime people work, the less engaged they are and the more likely they are to leave. A correlation is a single number, and one broken batch of data can hide a real relationship inside it. A chart shows the broken batch at a glance.

That's why every association in Module 2 starts with a chart, and why you'll ask your AI analyst for the chart and the number together.

What question does each chart answer?

Question first, chart second. The FT's Visual Vocabulary and Few's seven message types sort charts by the question they answer. Here they are side by side, with what each needs from your variables.

The questionFT familyUsual chartsVariables it needs
How has it changed over time?Change over timeLine, columnA date, and a count or measurement
Which is biggest? In what order?Ranking · MagnitudeSorted bar, dot plotA label, and a count, measurement, or rate
How far is each from a target or average?DeviationDiverging barA label, and a measurement against a reference
How are the values spread out?DistributionHistogram, box plot; counts by level for ordered labelsOne measurement, count, or ordered label
What share is each part of the whole?Part-to-wholeStacked bar, 100% bar (pies only for two or three parts)A label, and counts that add up
Do two things move together?CorrelationScatter plot; a gradient of rates by levelTwo measurements or counts, or an ordered label and a yes/no

The kind of variable still decides what you can do, as in Module 1. You can't draw a histogram of a label, and a bar of average ratings hides the spread a chart of counts by level would show.

The pattern: half the traps are about the denominator. Counts measure size; rates measure performance.

Grouping: what a group average hides

Most management questions compare groups: sites, departments, programs, tiers. A group's average can mislead in three ways.

1. The group's average can be one member

In Module 1's Online 1, leadership asked whether urban, suburban, or rural sites have more turnover. Here is the answer by site type, and then by site.

Site typeShare leaving per quarterRows
Urban9.9%1,040
Suburban13.4%1,177
Rural12.0%998
Urban siteShare leaving per quarterRows
S14.7%551
S215.7%489

"Urban sites retain staff best" is really "S1 retains staff best." Leave S1 out and the urban rate is 15.7%, the highest of the three. The grouping hid a difference inside the group, and S1 is also one of the three SmartShift sites. Keep that in mind for Online 2.

2. Counts measure size, not performance

Which Riverbend site misses the most client visits? By weekly count, the biggest ones: S1 and S2 miss about 5.3 and 5.5 visits a week, and S8 misses 3.2. But S1 has about 69 staff and S8 has 38. Per 10 staff, missed visits are between 0.75 and 0.90 a week at every site. Across sites, missed visits correlate 0.95 with staff on the roster. The ranking by count was a ranking by size.

3. Who is in the group changes the group's average

You met this in Module 1. Riverbend's average engagement was 3.11 per row but 2.80 per employee, because people who stay longer answer more surveys and are more engaged. The Berkeley admissions case from the reading is the same idea: men and women applied to different departments, and the departments admitted at different rates.

Three rules for any grouped comparison

  1. Say what the groups are, and why those groups.
  2. Show the count in each group. Small groups give unstable averages.
  3. Compare rates, not totals, and then look one level down.

Activity: regroup it (25 minutes, pods)

Each card is a finding someone wants to put in a report. For each: what could the grouping hide, and how would you regroup to check?

Nonprofit

  1. "Students in our after-school program graduate at 82%, against 74% across the district."
  2. "Volunteers we recruit online stay longer than volunteers we recruit at events."

Public

  1. "Average days to close a case rose 20% this year."
  2. "District A has the most code violations in the county."

Private

  1. "Store B has the highest sales per employee."
  2. "Customers who use our app spend 30% more."
Some answers (after you've tried)
  1. Who enrolls: families who sign up for an after-school program may differ from the district as a whole. Compare students with similar grades and schools.
  2. Online recruiting may have started recently, or for different roles. Compare within role and start year.
  3. The mix of cases may have changed, with more of the slow kind. Compare by case type.
  4. District A may simply have the most properties. Use violations per 1,000 properties.
  5. Store B may have more part-time staff. Use sales per hour worked.
  6. Heavy shoppers may be the ones who download the app. Compare customers with similar spending before the app.

Cards 1, 2, and 6 are also about who chose to be in the group. Hold on to that: it's the heart of Online 2 and of Module 3.

Association, three shapes

Two variables are associated when knowing one tells you something about the other. The kinds of the two variables decide what the association looks like and how to show it.

Each step up the scale, fewer people leave. It's the clearest picture of association in the dataset, and it needs no average of a rating. Notice the blanks: people who didn't answer leave more often than anyone who answered 2 or higher.

Correlation, in words

A correlation is one number between −1 and +1 for how closely two measurements move together along a straight line. You never need the formula. Read it in words:

  • Direction: positive (both rise together) or negative (one rises as the other falls).
  • Strength, roughly: near 0, little straight-line relationship; around 0.2, weak; around 0.4, moderate; 0.6 and beyond, strong. These are rough guides, not rules.
  • What one number can't show: a curve, a few extreme points, a broken batch of data, or groups mixed together. Healy's Anscombe quartet and tonight's S6 rows are why you always look at the scatter.

And what no association can show: which one causes the other, or whether something else causes both. That's Online 2's subject, and Module 3's.
