The demo, step by step

These are the prompts used live. Paste each into your own Gemini chat. Think about the question in bold before you open "What we found." Gemini's charts and wording will differ from ours, but the numbers should match. If a number doesn't, that's a check worth running.

D1

Ask for a chart for a question, not a chart of a variable

Leadership asks: do sites with more client referrals have longer intake waits? Which chart would answer that, using site_weeks? Draw it, and tell me what one dot represents.

What is one dot: a site-week, a site, or a month? Does the answer change with the choice?

What we found

The easy answer is a scatter plot of all 832 site-weeks, with a correlation of about 0.25. But big sites have both more referrals and somewhat longer waits, so that cloud mixes "big site vs. small site" with "busy week vs. quiet week." It answered a question, but not obviously leadership's. Step D5 takes the level apart. Before you ask for a chart, say what one dot should be. It's Module 1's "what is a row?" again.

Posted after November 1

D3

A grouped comparison, and one level down

Compare the share of staff_quarters rows with left_org = 1 for full-time and part-time staff. Then break it down by role. Show the number of rows in every group.

Does the overall comparison hold inside every role? Which group would you not trust, and why?

What we found

Overall, part-time staff leave less often: 8.9% of rows against 12.8%. Within most roles that holds. Among case managers it flips: 24.0% of part-timers leave against 15.1% of full-timers. But there are only 25 part-time case-manager rows, so six departures make the 24%. That's rule 2 from the kick-off: show the count in each group, and don't build a finding on a handful of rows.

D4

Engagement and leaving: averages or a gradient?

Is engagement associated with leaving? Show me the answer two ways: the average engagement of people who left and people who stayed, and the share who left at each engagement level from 1 to 5. Chart the second one.

Which answer would you show your board?

What we found

The averages: 2.50 for leavers, 3.18 for stayers. True, but it averages an ordered label, and it hides the shape. The gradient shows it: 27.7% leave at level 1, then 13.6%, 9.1%, 5.3%, and 2.9% at level 5. Ask for the gradient, not the averages, when one variable is an ordered label.

D5

Referrals and waits, level by level

Using site_weeks, give me the correlation between cases_opened and median_intake_wait_days three ways: (1) week to week within each site, after removing each site's own average; (2) across the eight site averages; (3) month by month, averaging all sites. Explain in plain words why they differ.

Which level matches leadership's question? Which matches Little's Law from Projects at the boundary?

What we found
LevelCorrelation
All 832 site-weeks together0.25
Week to week, within a site0.07
Across the eight sites0.48
Month by month, all sites0.80

The same two columns give four answers. Week to week, a busy week barely moves the wait. Across the year, waits rise with the December–January surge in referrals (December waits average 5.6 days against about 4.3 in spring), which is what Little's Law predicts when arrivals outrun capacity. Across sites, the association mixes in size. An association belongs to a level. That's Freedman's point, and it's why your Results draft must say which level yours is at.

D6

Is SmartShift associated with lower turnover?

Is SmartShift associated with lower turnover? Compare the sites that adopted it (S1, S4, S7) with the others.
Now show the same comparison separately for 2024, before SmartShift, and 2025, after it went live.

What did the second prompt show that the first one hid?

What we found
Share leaving per quarter2024 (before)2025 (after)
SmartShift sites10.4%5.0%
Other sites15.3%14.0%

The first answer is true: 7.7% against 14.6% over both years. But the SmartShift sites had lower turnover before SmartShift existed. Some of the gap was already there. How much of the rest is SmartShift? That's Online 2's question.

Activity: what a correlation looks like (15 minutes)

Open Kristoffer Magnusson's interactive correlation slider. It draws a scatter plot for any correlation you choose. Work alone or with a partner.

  1. Set it to 0.2, then 0.5, then 0.8. Which of the three could you spot by eye without the number? Riverbend's intake waits and client satisfaction correlate at about −0.7 across site-weeks (negative: the same picture, tilted the other way).
  2. Set it to 0.2 and look hard. Would you tell your board that these two things are related? What would you say instead?
  3. Drag one point far from the rest. Watch the correlation change. How many points does it take to hide, or fake, a relationship?

Write one sentence for your reading notebook: what can a correlation tell you that a scatter plot can't, and what can the scatter plot tell you that the number can't?

Your turn: the prompt card

About 25 minutes. Work alone or with a partner. Run your two checks on every chart.

1

A chart for a question

Which Riverbend sites have the most documentation errors? Answer with a chart, and tell me whether you used counts or a rate, and why.

Check: did it use counts? Ask for errors per 10 staff, and see whether the ranking changes.

2

A gradient

For staff_quarters, show the share of rows with left_org = 1 at each job_level, with the number of rows at each level. Chart it.

Write one sentence: is there a gradient, and is every level big enough to trust?

Then ask it one more thing: "Would this association look different at another level, or within groups?" Keep the chart and the answer. They're the first draft of your Results.
