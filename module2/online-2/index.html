Five reasons two things move together

When two things move together, one causing the other is only one of the possibilities. Before your Results section says anything about cause, go through all five.

ReasonWhat's happeningAn example
1. CausationOne really does drive the otherIf overtime wears people down and worn-down people leave, overtime and leaving will move together for this reason.
2. Reverse direction or timingThe "outcome" drives the "cause," or the order of events is offCustomers who have decided to cancel stop opening the newsletter, so "reads the newsletter" seems to keep customers.
3. A common causeSomething else drives bothIce cream sales and drownings both rise in summer. Stores in busy locations have both more staff and more sales.
4. Selection or groupingWho is in the data, or how it's grouped, creates the patternCustomers who choose the app were already big spenders. Berkeley's departments.
5. CoincidenceTwo trends that happen to line up, or a pattern in too few rowsVigen's spurious correlations. Six departures among 25 part-time case-manager rows, from Online 1.

And a sixth that isn't about the world at all: a data artifact, one of the five ways data lies. A definition changes, a batch is mismeasured, and two columns move together because of how they were recorded.

That's the debate in the readings. Anderson says that with enough data, correlation is enough. Harford and Lazer's Google Flu Trends is what happens when a correlation with no model behind it meets a world that changes. A correlation can't tell you when it will stop working, because it doesn't know why it worked.

The demo: SmartShift, step by step

In Online 1, the SmartShift sites had lower turnover, but they already did in 2024. Tonight we push further. Paste each prompt into your Riverbend chat. Think about the question in bold before you open "What we found."

This step is posted after the Module 1 exercise closes on Sunday, November 1.

D3

Who answered the survey

For each quarter, what share of staff_quarters rows have an engagement answer rather than a blank? Compare SmartShift sites with the others.

If SmartShift sites' engagement looks higher in 2025, is it the same kind of group answering?

What we found

Response rose at every site, from about 61% of rows in early 2024 to 70% by late 2025, and it was higher at the SmartShift sites throughout (about 70% of rows in 2024 and 78% in 2025, against 56% and 63% elsewhere). When more people answer, the people answering change, and an average of their answers can move even if no one's engagement did. That's selected.

D4

What else changed?

Compare 2024Q4 with 2025Q1 for every column in staff_quarters and site_weeks, at all sites. List everything that changed noticeably at once, at every site, not just at S1, S4, and S7.

Each thing on the list is a rival explanation for any 2025 change you'd like to credit to SmartShift. Which would you check first?

So, does SmartShift work?

Riverbend's data shows an association: the SmartShift sites had lower turnover in 2025. It cannot yet tell you how much of that SmartShift caused, because:

  • the SmartShift sites were already different before it arrived (a common cause, or selection: who chose those three sites, and why?);
  • other things changed at the same time, across Riverbend (a data artifact, or a rival cause);
  • the people answering the survey changed (selection);
  • some site differences are large enough to matter and small enough to be chance.

What would you need to know? Write one sentence before Module 3. A hint: comparing the SmartShift sites' levels to other sites' levels gets you the first problem. What if you compared their changes? That, and "who chose S1, S4, and S7?", is where Module 3 begins.

This course has the same problem. It's a redesign with no comparable baseline and more than one change at once. If students do well this year, how much of it is the redesign? You now know the questions to ask.

The Results draft

One to two pages. It becomes the Results section of your white paper, so it is not extra work. Copy the outline into your own document and fill it in during the work session.

RESULTS (draft) 1. The question and the prediction My question (and its type: descriptive, exploratory, inferential, predictive, or causal). What my model predicts the data will show. 2. The chart (one or two) For each: "This chart answers: ______." The kinds of the variables, and what one dot or bar represents. 3. The association In plain words: its direction, roughly how strong, the number of rows behind it, and the level (people, sites, weeks, months). 4. Against the model Consistent, inconsistent, or can't tell yet, and why. If consistent: one other model that would predict the same thing. 5. What this does not establish The strongest other explanation (reverse direction, a common cause, selection or grouping, coincidence, or a data artifact). My data concern from Data & Methods section 4, and what it does here. Whether my decision needs a model that explains or one that predicts.

Work session: four steps

Replace anything in [brackets] with your own project's details. The AI does the arithmetic; every sentence about what it means is yours.

1

Check the chart against its question

Here is my question: [your question]. Here is my chart: [describe it, or upload it]. Does this chart answer that question? Is there a chart that would answer it more directly, given that my variables are [kinds]?

Use what your pod wrote at the gallery walk. Where did readers misread the question or the answer?

2

Test the association

My finding is: [your association]. Check it three ways: (1) does it hold within the main groups in my data, such as [a grouping]? (2) does it hold at a different level, such as [people / sites / months]? (3) does it depend on a few rows or one batch of data? Show the numbers and the counts.

If it changes within groups or across levels, that's not a failure. It's your finding, stated more precisely.

3

Name the rivals

My data shows that [your association]. For each of these, say whether it could explain the association instead of [the cause I have in mind], and what I would expect to see in my data if it did: reverse direction or timing, a common cause, selection or grouping, coincidence, and a change in how the data was recorded.

Which rival is strongest? That one goes in section 5. Check whether the AI invented evidence for any of them.

4

Say exactly what you found

Here is my Results paragraph: [paste it]. Mark every word or phrase that claims more than an association, such as "drives," "reduces," "leads to," "because," or "the effect of." For each, suggest wording that says exactly what the data shows, and tell me what evidence would justify the stronger claim.

Hernán's rule: say plainly if your question is causal. Then say honestly how far this evidence gets you.

Before you submit: the common failures

These are the problems most likely to show up in Results drafts. Check yours against each one.

FailureWhat it looks likeThe fix
A chart without a questionA chart is included because the AI made it.Write "This chart answers: …" above every chart. If you can't finish the sentence, cut the chart.
Totals, not ratesThe biggest group "has the most problems."Divide by the right denominator: per person, per case, per 1,000.
Wrong levelSite averages used to claim something about people, or the reverse.Name the level in section 3, and say whether it's the level your model is about.
A correlation without a chart"r = 0.3" and nothing else.Show the scatter or the gradient. One number hides curves, clusters, and broken batches.
Silent choicesRows, blanks, or a batch dropped without saying so.Report what you dropped, how many, and why.
A causal verb"Overtime drives turnover" from an association.Say what the data shows. Save the causal claim, and the evidence for it, for Module 3.
