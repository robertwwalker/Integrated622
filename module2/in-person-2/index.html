Where the Results draft stands

The Results draft is due at the Module 3 kick-off. Its five sections: the question and the prediction; the chart; the association; how it compares with your model; and what it does not establish. The full template is on the Online 2 page. Tonight builds sections 2 through 4, and starts section 5.

Activity: the gallery walk (50 minutes, pods)

Each member puts out one chart, with no title and no explanation. Pod-mates walk the charts and leave three notes on each:

1. The question

What question does this chart answer?

2. The answer

What does it say the answer is?

3. The limit

What can't it tell me?

Then each author reads their notes and compares them with what they meant. Where readers got the question wrong, the chart needs a different form or a title that states the question. Where they got the answer wrong, it needs a different scale, grouping, or denominator. Note one change you'll make.

Back to the model

In Module 1, Box described learning as a loop: the model says what to look for, the data pushes back, the model improves. Your finding is the data pushing back. For each finding, decide which of three it is:

VerdictMeansWhat to write
ConsistentThe data looks the way your model predictedConsistent with the model, which is not the same as proving it. Name another model that would predict the same thing.
InconsistentThe data looks different from the predictionWhich part of the model is wrong, or which way the data might be lying? This is often the most useful finding.
Can't tell yetThe data can't separate your prediction from othersWhat data, or what comparison, would let you tell?

A worked example: Little's Law at Riverbend

Projects at the boundary made a prediction from Little's Law: if referrals rise and staffing doesn't, waits should rise. What does Riverbend's data say?

  • Over the seasons, consistent. Month by month, referrals and median waits move together strongly (a correlation of 0.80, from Online 1). The winter surge brings the longest waits.
  • Week to week within a site, can't tell. The correlation is near zero, 0.07. A single busy week may not be enough to build a queue.
  • When capacity falls, consistent. The two sites whose open positions rose most in 2025 saw waits rise too. At S1, open positions went from 0.8 to 1.8 on average and the median wait from 4.8 to 5.3 days; at S2, open positions doubled from 2.5 to 5.3 and the wait went from 5.4 to 5.7. At most other sites, both held roughly steady.

Three verdicts, one model. The model held where its conditions held, and the data was silent where they didn't. That's what an honest Results section sounds like.

Your turn (10 minutes, alone)

Write the verdict for your own finding, in one sentence, with its reason. If it's "consistent," name one other model that would predict the same thing. Box would call the one that worries you most the tiger.

Explaining vs. predicting

Kleinberg and his coauthors' two decision-makers: one wonders whether to pay for a rain dance, the other whether to carry an umbrella. The rain dance is a causal question: would acting change the outcome? The umbrella only needs a prediction: will it rain? Different questions need different models, as Shmueli argues.

A model that explainsA model that predicts
The questionWhy does this happen? What if we change something?Who, which, when, how many?
Judged byWhether it gets the cause rightWhether it's right about cases it hasn't seen
Its variablesChosen by your model of the organizationWhatever predicts, cause or not
On Pearl's ladderNeeds the second rungThe first rung is enough

A predictive model of who leaves Riverbend

Here is a model predicting left_org from what Riverbend knows each quarter: engagement, overtime, caseload, tenure, training, role, full- or part-time, pay, commute, and site.

Build a model that predicts left_org in staff_quarters from engagement, overtime_hours, caseload, tenure_years, training_completed, role, employment_type, pay_hourly, commute_miles, and site_id. Fit it on 2024 only and test it on 2025. In plain words: if I pick one person who left and one who stayed, how often does the model rank the leaver as riskier? Which predictors matter most?
What we found
  • It predicts moderately well. Pick one person who left and one who stayed at random: the model ranks the leaver as riskier about 7 times in 10. Fit on 2024 alone, it does a little worse on 2025, a year it never saw (about 2 times in 3), but still far better than a coin toss. That's the umbrella test, and it passes. (Statisticians call this number the AUC; 0.5 is a coin toss and 1.0 is perfect.)
  • Overtime alone, or engagement alone, does nearly as well: each picks the leaver about two times in three.
  • One catch: every predictor here was measured in the same quarter the person left. A list for the start of a quarter can only use what was known before it began. How much of the 7 in 10 survives? That's Part 5 of the Module 2 exercise.
  • Commute distance predicts nothing. Leavers and stayers have nearly the same median commute: 11.5 miles against 11.2. A null finding is a finding.

Predictors that aren't levers

A predictive model uses whatever predicts. That doesn't make every predictor something a manager can pull.

  • Training completed is lower among leavers: 29% of their rows against 40% for stayers, in every role. Does training keep people? Before you say so, name two other explanations. It's tonight's warm-up for the Skeptic's Chair.

So: a leaving model, built on what's known in advance, can decide whom to check in with next quarter: the umbrella. Don't use it to decide whether more training or less overtime would keep people, the rain dance. That second question is Module 3's.

Activity: the question sort (20 minutes, pairs)

Sort each leadership question: predict (who, which, when, how many) or explain and act (why, and what happens if we change something)? One card belongs in both. Find it.

Nonprofit

  1. Which donors are most likely to lapse next year?
  2. Would moving volunteer training online keep volunteers longer?
  3. How many clients will we serve next January?
  4. Did the new intake form shorten waits?

Public

  1. Which permit applications will take more than 60 days?
  2. Why do some districts close cases faster than others?
  3. Which restaurant inspections are most likely to find violations?
  4. Would raising caseworker pay reduce turnover?

Private

  1. Which subscribers will cancel this quarter?
  2. Will a loyalty discount increase repeat purchases?
  3. Which new hires are likely to leave in their first year?
  4. Which staff should managers check in with next quarter, and will the check-ins keep them?
Check your sort (after you've tried)
  • Predict: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11.
  • Explain and act: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10.
  • Both: 12. "Which staff" is an umbrella; "will the check-ins keep them" is a rain dance. Many real decisions are like this: a prediction decides where to act, and a causal question decides whether acting works.

Card 6 is worth a second look: "why" sounds explanatory, but a manager asking it usually wants to know what to change. Which card is closest to your own project?

Project workshop: the Skeptic's Chair (45 minutes, pods)

Each Author presents their finding: the chart, the association, and the verdict against their model. This time the Skeptic names the strongest other explanation for the association, from this list:

Other explanationThe Skeptic asks
ChanceHow many rows are behind it? Would it survive another quarter's data?
Grouping or levelWould it hold within groups, or at another level?
A common causeIs there something that drives both?
Reverse direction or timingCould the outcome be driving the "cause"?
A data artifactWhich of the five ways data lies could produce it?

Roles rotate as before: Author (3 min), Translator (is the chart's form right for the question?), Skeptic, and Scribe. The Author gets the last word, and writes down the strongest objection. It goes in section 5 of the Results draft.
