The Skeptic: a first critique from Gemini

Run each draft through Gemini before a person reads it, then read a pod-mate's draft with one short form

About 15 minutes for the SkepticAbout 20 minutes per pod review

What this is

Before any draft goes to your pod, you run it through the Skeptic: a Gemini prompt that reads your draft as a careful decision-maker would and tells you where it would stop, doubt, or misread. It is a first reader that is always available. It is not a grader, it does not rewrite, and it can be wrong. Your pod-mate is the second reader, and a person reads for things the AI cannot see: your organization, your industry, and what is plausible.

Posting the report with the draft lets your reviewer see what the AI already caught, so they can spend their time on what it missed.

How to run it (every gate)

  1. Open Gemini signed in with your Willamette account, and start a new chat.
  2. Copy the prompt for your gate (below) and paste it into Gemini.
  3. Paste your draft where the prompt says to. Paste the text of the draft, not a link. Remove names and confidential details first about your workplace; your employer's rules still apply.
  4. Read the report. Disagree wherever it is wrong. If something it says is unclear, ask it.
  5. Copy the report into a Google Doc titled "Skeptic report, Gate N," share it with comment access from your Willamette account, and post the link with your draft in your pod's Pod notes and drafts discussion in Canvas (People, Groups, your pod, Discussions).

Gate 1: the Project Brief

The rubric criteria this prompt looks for: B1 to B7, in the rubric in Canvas under Files, Rubrics.

You are the Skeptic: a critical first reader for a graduate business student's project draft. You tell the author where a careful decision-maker, or a careful colleague, would stop, doubt, or misread. You are not a grader and you do not rewrite. Rules: - Use only what is in the draft. Do not invent facts about the organization, the data, or the author's field. If a point depends on context you cannot see, say so under "What I cannot judge." - Quote the exact words (15 words or fewer) of each passage you object to, so the author can find it. - Give at most four objections, ranked from most to least serious. Label each one: unclear, unsupported, missing, or mismatched (two parts that do not fit together). - For each objection, say what would resolve it. Do not write the fix. - Do not give a grade or a score. Allow one sentence on what already works. - Use plain words. Do not use terms the author did not use. - If the draft is too short or too vague to judge a point, say so instead of guessing. Write your report in exactly these five parts: 1. The claim as I read it: one sentence. 2. Objections (up to four, ranked): the quoted words; the label; why it matters; what would resolve it. 3. One question the author should be able to answer in a sentence but could not from this draft. 4. What I cannot judge from the page. 5. What already works: one sentence. This draft is a Project Brief. It should have six elements: the organization (real, representative, or a useful fiction; the sector; what it is doing or struggling with); the model (key concepts and the relationships between them, and what would have to be true for the model to be right); the question (one change, decision, intervention, or comparison to evaluate); the prediction (which variable moves, in which direction, stated before looking at data); the data sketch (the variables needed, and whether the author has the data, could find it, or would simulate it); and the disciplinary anchor (the frameworks or findings from the author's prior course). Pay particular attention to these four things. Is the model a set of relationships, or only a topic? Does the question follow from the model and name a comparison? Could the prediction fail in any data, or is it too vague to be wrong? Does the data sketch measure what the prediction is about? Here is the draft: [paste your draft below this line]

Pay particular attention to these four things. Is each variable's kind right, and is each "what it misses" specific to that variable? Does any summary not fit the variable's kind (for example, an average of an ordered label)? If the data is simulated, were the relationships taken from the model or tuned toward the answer? This stage claims only an association. Flag any word that claims more than that, such as "drives," "reduces," "leads to," or "the effect of," and say what evidence would be needed to justify it. Pay particular attention to these four things. Does each chart answer a stated question? Is the count and the level named? Is the strongest other explanation specific, with what the author would see if it were true? Does the draft say which kind of model the decision needs, one that explains or one that predicts? Gate 4: the pre-final version

Reading a pod-mate's draft: the review form

Your pod-mate posts a draft and its Skeptic report in your pod's Pod notes and drafts discussion in Canvas. You review one draft at each gate, about 20 minutes, in this order: read the draft first, write field 1, then open the Skeptic report. Comment in the author's document (Commenting, not Suggesting). Then post your answers to the five fields as a reply under the author's post, so the author sees them, and paste the same answers into the Canvas review box for credit. Pods support; they do not co-author, so do not rewrite their text.

FieldWhat goes in it
1. The claim, in one sentence, as you read it.Write this before you open the Skeptic report, so the report does not shape your reading.
2. Strongest objection, and where.Quote the words (15 words or fewer) or name the section. Required.
3. One thing to keep.Something that already works and should not be lost.
4. One specific change.What you would change and why. A change, not a rewrite.
5. Optional: where you disagree with the Skeptic report.If you do not, you can skip this.

Each gate adds two questions of its own:

GateTwo more questions
Gate 1: the Project Brief1. Does the prediction follow from the model, and could it fail in any data? Name the variable, the direction, and what would count as a surprise.
2. Does the data sketch measure what the prediction is about? If not, name the mismatch.
Gate 2: the Data & Methods draft1. Pick one variable in the table. Is its kind right, and is 'what it misses' specific to that variable rather than generic?
2. Does the quality concern say what you would actually see in the data if it were happening?
Gate 3: the Results draft1. Find the strongest word in the draft ('drives,' 'reduces,' 'leads to'). Does the evidence support it? If not, what would you write instead?
2. Is the strongest other explanation specific, with what the author would expect to see in the data if it were true?

What a specific comment looks like

Generic: "Be more specific about your model."
Specific: "In paragraph 2 you write "engagement drives retention." Which moves first, and over what period? If retention is measured once a year, how would you see engagement change before it?"

Credit is for completing every field on time and for specificity: an objection with the words it points to. It is not for agreeing with the author, and not for whether the author takes your advice. If a review is more than 48 hours late, the next pod-mate writes it, so no author is left without one. If the draft is not posted, or you cannot open it, by the deadline, email me and you will not lose credit.

Your "what I changed" line

In your next submission, after your note on how you used AI, add one line: "From the Skeptic report, my pod-mate's review, and my pod's discussion I changed: ___ (and where it came from: Gemini, my pod-mate, our pod meeting, or my own idea)." It is not graded. It shows what worked, so we can keep the parts of this process that help.
