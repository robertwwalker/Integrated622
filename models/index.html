The idea behind the course

Models and the MBA

Every discipline in the MBA is a model. Strategy links them. Data is where they meet reality.

An MBA is strategy, built from models

At its heart, an MBA is training in strategy: deciding where an organization should go and how it will get there. But strategy isn't one subject. It's built from parts, and every part is a model.

  • Finance models how value is created and how risk is priced.
  • Accounting models the organization as a system of resources and claims.
  • Operations models how work flows and where it backs up.
  • Organizational management models why people do what they do inside organizations.
  • Marketing models how customers choose.
  • Human resources models how talent is attracted, developed, and kept.
  • Information systems model how an organization's knowledge is captured and moves.
  • Data and decision science model uncertainty: how evidence is generated, and how to decide when you can't be sure.

Each is a simplified picture of one part of the organization, and each is useful precisely because of what it leaves out. Strategy is the work of linking those models into a coherent whole.

Consider a decision to open a new site. It is a marketing model (will demand be there?), an operations model (can we serve it?), a finance model (does it pay?), and an organizational model (who will run it, and will they stay?). Coherent strategy is what you get when the models agree. Good managers notice when they don't.

Where this course fits: data is the measurement of models

Data and decision science is one of those models. It is also something more: data is where every other model meets reality. Each model makes predictions, and data is the measurement that tells you whether they hold. That's why this course is not another silo. It is the discipline that lets you test the others, and use them to look ahead.

It's also why the course comes after organizational or operations management, and is integrated with them. You arrive with a model. Here, you learn to measure it.

What is a model?

A model is a simplified, explicit statement of how something works. Every model has four parts, and you've already written all four in your Project Brief:

PartThe questionIn your Project Brief
ConceptsWhat are the moving pieces?Element 2, the model
RelationshipsWhat affects what, and in which direction?Element 2
AssumptionsWhat must be true for this to hold? Where does it apply?Element 2: "what would have to be true"
PredictionsIf the model is right, what would we see?Element 4, the prediction

Four degrees of explicitness

The course moves you up this ladder:

  1. A mental model: implicit; the intuition you already run on.
  2. A stated model: in words. "When workload rises, engagement falls, because…" That's your Brief.
  3. A diagram: boxes and arrows. In Module 3 this becomes a causal diagram.
  4. A formal model: an equation or a simulation. AI makes this reachable without code.

What makes a model useful to a manager

Explicit

Others can see it and argue with it.

Testable

It predicts something you could observe.

Decision-relevant

It would change what you do.

Simple

You can hold it in your head.

Scoped

It says where it applies, and where it doesn't.

Honest

All models are wrong. A useful one says how.

Two jobs for every model: testing and forecasting

A model does two different jobs, and a manager needs both.

JobThe questionWhich way it looks
TestingIs the model telling the truth?Backward: compare what the model predicts with data that already exists.
ForecastingWhat will happen next, or if we act?Forward: run the model on situations no one has observed yet.

Decisions are about the future, so managers live on forecasts. Academic research leans toward testing, because tests are what get published, and it's easy to come away from a statistics course thinking testing is all models are for. It isn't. A model you have never tested is risky to forecast with. But a model you have tested and never used to look ahead is a model no one acted on.

Three kinds of forecast

Prediction

If nothing changes, what happens next? Next quarter's turnover at current trends.

Scenario

If conditions change, what happens? If referrals rise 15% this winter, how long will intake waits get?

Intervention

If we act, what happens? If we roll the new schedule out everywhere, what turnover should we expect? This is the "doing" rung of Pearl's ladder in Module 3.

Simulation and AI make forecasting reachable. Once your model is stated, an AI analyst can run it forward under as many scenarios as you can describe, in plain language. An honest forecast always comes with a range, not a single number. The width of the range tells you how much your model, and your data, actually know.

Two models, not one

Here is the idea the whole course turns on. A statistical model is also a model: a model of how the data came to be. So every project here holds two models, and the work is moving between them.

MoveDirectionWhere you do it
OperationalizeFrom the model of the organization to the model of the data: what gets recorded, and what kind of thing it isModule 1
SimulateForward, from the model to the data it would produceModule 1
AnalyzeBackward, from the data to the models that could have produced itModule 2
Reason causallyWhich models of the organization the data can tell apart, and which it can'tModule 3
ForecastForward again, under scenarios: what will happen if conditions change, or if we act. A tested model makes a more trustworthy forecast.Modules 1 and 3, and the recommendation in your white paper

This is also why simulation is honest work, not fabrication. A simulation is a model of how the data came to be, stated openly and run forward. The Riverbend dataset is exactly that: data generated from a stated model of a fictional organization. Your job over three modules is to work backward from it.
