Your pod, Canvas, and this site, on one page

What a pod is

A pod is three or four classmates from your own location and track who read your project as it grows. They read your drafts, ask the hard question, and hear your talk. They do not co-author: every project is individual. Pod feedback counts toward participation, and the Canvas Participation page gives the details.

How pods work, so absences never stall them

  • Any two people can run a pod meeting. If someone is missing, the rest carry on and leave a short written note for the absent person.
  • Each draft is read by a named pod-mate, with a second pod-mate ready as backup. You also run each draft through Gemini for a first critique before a person reads it. The prompt and the one-page review form are on the Skeptic page; we will walk through them at the kick-off.
  • Whatever is said live is also written down. The written note is what counts.
  • Your pod's list, which sets who reviews whom and whom you reply to on the Thursday boards, is pinned in that discussion.

Your first deliverable: the Project Brief

Due before your first in-person night: Tue Oct 20 (Salem) · Wed Oct 21 (Portland), 6:00 pmRubric: Canvas → Files → Rubrics

Your prior module has been building toward something. Write this brief as that module concludes, when your thinking is most developed. You are not starting from zero; you are arriving with a partially formed project. Your pod will be your first readers.

One to two pages, covering six elements:

1. The organization

Real, representative, or a useful fiction? What sector? What is it doing, trying to do, or struggling with?

2. The model

What model has your prior coursework given you about how part of this organization works? Name the key variables and the relationships between them. What would have to be true for the model to be right?

3. The question

What change, decision, intervention, or comparison do you want to evaluate?

4. The prediction

If your model is right, what would you expect to see in data? In what direction? What does movement in your key variable look like?

5. The data sketch

What data would test your prediction? Do you have it, could you find it, or would you simulate it? Name the variables you would need.

6. The disciplinary anchor

Which frameworks, concepts, or findings from your prior course drive this? What are you bringing into the integration?

How the Brief grows into your final project: see The project.

Subject: Your Integrated Course — What Comes Next · Organizational Management

I promised to take the first step. Here it is.

Everything for this course lives at the course website — https://robertwwalker.github.io/Integrated622/ — including the three videos, with one addressed specifically to where you're coming from.

Three modules, one idea: data is the measurement of models. Each module runs roughly two weeks, with the final project due December 3. We don't collect data and wonder what it means. We build models of how organizations work, and data is how we find out whether those models are telling us the truth.

  • Module 1 — Data in organizations: vocabulary, variable types, structured and unstructured data across public, private, and nonprofit sectors.
  • Module 2 — What you can do with data: visualization, grouping, association. What each approach can answer, and what it can't.
  • Module 3 — Causation: Judea Pearl's causal framework. Not just what happened, but why.

One note on timing: the third module is split by Thanksgiving week. We finish the content before the break — no deadlines hanging over the holiday, wherever you spend it. That week is for reflection. We return to complete the module and submit the final project on December 3.

The project: A model of some aspect of an organization, your discipline's lens applied to it, and either real data or honest simulation to evaluate it. Simulation is not fabrication — it is a rigorous tradition in supply chain, risk modeling, and public health. The intellectual work is in the model design.

The tools: AI — Claude, Gemini — as analytical partners. Plain language. No code. The tools handle the technical work. Your job is the thinking — what to ask, what the answer means, what the data cannot tell you. That is exactly the work your training has been building toward.

A word specifically for you.

You arrive with mental models of organizations — frameworks, intuitions, observations built through your coursework. Those models make predictions. Data is how we find out whether they're telling us the truth.

The hardest work won't be the statistics. It will be translation: taking a concept like employee engagement or change readiness and deciding what it looks like as a variable. What would you measure? How would you know you're measuring the right thing? That is a conceptual challenge, not a technical one — and it is exactly what your training has prepared you for.

Your instinct that numbers don't capture everything is also correct. Pearl's framework in the third module vindicates it: the difference between two things moving together and one actually causing the other is exactly what good organizational thinking needs to hold onto.

And here is something worth naming: you are studying organizational change — and you are inside one. This course is itself a redesign. Two simultaneous changes, a non-comparable cohort, an imperfect baseline. We face exactly the question we're teaching you to ask: did it work? We don't know yet. That is not a disclaimer. It is the point.

You are arriving with a partially formed project — one your organizational management coursework has been developing. The foundation is there. What this course provides is the means to operationalize it: to define the variables, establish what you would measure, and bring your model into contact with data. We are starting from what you already know.

If you haven't yet watched the video addressed to organizational management students, it is at the course website — worth watching before our first session.

Please make sure to contact me so that we can ensure that you're comfortable with things and on the right track (rwalker@willamette.edu | Calendar open | Google Meet available).

When you reach out, tell me where you're coming from and what you're curious about — or what you're uncertain about. Either is a fine beginning.

Something worth knowing about where you stand.

You arrive with something most students don't have: a quantitative foundation already at home with data. You understand that processes generate evidence, that evidence should inform decisions, and that the gap between what we expect and what we observe is where learning happens. That is exactly the instinct this course is trying to cultivate.

You also arrive with your organizational management foundation. Three disciplines where others have two. Your project asks you to bring an operational lens and an organizational lens together under a question that data can speak to — and that intersection is where the most interesting management questions always live.

For you, AI doesn't remove the floor — it removes the ceiling. The sophistication of the analysis you can engage with is no longer limited by what you can build from scratch. It is limited by the quality of your questions. And from where you stand — with three foundations — those questions can be genuinely wide.

I still teach panel data at the Essex Summer School for Social Science Data Analysis — the technique for tracking how things change over time across multiple units. It is precisely the methodological question this course is asking about itself. You have the foundations to see that clearly and engage with it seriously.

You are arriving with a partially formed project — questions your operations coursework has already been shaping about processes, performance, and the gap between expectation and outcome. The foundation is there. Three foundations mean the operationalization can go deeper than any single discipline had room to take it. We are starting from everything you have built.
